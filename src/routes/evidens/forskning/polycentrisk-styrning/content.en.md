# Polycentric Governance

## When can multiple relatively autonomous decision centres outperform a single centre?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported
**Confidence:** Medium
**Key finding:** Autonomous local units can achieve real capacity and specialization gains through voluntary cooperation without merging into a single centre — but this requires trust, formalization and relational infrastructure between the cooperating units, and it trades some direct accountability for that capacity.
**Coverage note:** Primary evidence is Swedish intermunicipal cooperation; genuinely international polycentric and natural-resource-governance cases are planned but not yet built.

---

## The question

Classical Ostromian polycentric governance theory proposes that multiple, formally independent decision centres, operating under an overarching set of rules, can outperform a single monopolistic authority. This topic asks:

> Under what conditions does a polycentric arrangement of autonomous local units actually work, and what does "working" require architecturally?

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Autonomy** — each unit retains its own decision-making authority rather than being subordinated to a central authority.
- **Voluntary coordination** — units choose to cooperate rather than being merged or commanded.
- **Relational capability** — trust, formalization, and interaction quality between cooperating units, which appears to be a real, separate success factor rather than a formality.
- **Accountability diffusion risk** — the more units share a function, the harder it becomes to answer "who is responsible?"

---

## What the evidence shows

**[Intermunicipal cooperation (S5)](/evidens/fall/sverige/mellankommunal-samverkan/)** is a genuine, large-scale, real-world instance of polycentric governance in the Ostromian sense: more than 9 in 10 Swedish municipalities report contractual cooperation, roughly 8 in 10 belong to a municipal federation, and nearly 7 in 10 to at least one joint committee — while each municipality retains its own political autonomy and electoral accountability. Statskontoret's research identifies **trust, good relations, interaction, formalization, and thorough preparation** as the key success factors distinguishing cooperation that works from cooperation that doesn't — polycentric arrangements don't function automatically just because the formal structure exists; they require what the project's R8 framework calls relational or inter-organizational capability.

The cost of this autonomy is real: cooperation can reduce individual municipalities' direct control over the shared function, and Statskontoret found many municipalities struggled to maintain a full overview of their own cooperation agreements — a concrete accountability-tracking problem, not just a theoretical risk.

---

## Where findings agree

- Polycentric cooperation between formally autonomous units can produce genuine capacity gains without centralizing political authority.
- The success of such arrangements depends heavily on relational infrastructure between units, not just the legal permission to cooperate.

## Where findings diverge

- The corpus does not yet have a case testing polycentric governance for a function with large ecological or resource-commons characteristics (the classic Ostrom domain) — Swedish intermunicipal cooperation is mostly administrative/service cooperation, not commons governance in the original sense.

---

## Counterevidence

- Accountability costs are real: cooperation measurably reduces direct municipal control and can create "who's responsible?" ambiguity, directly complicating a naive "polycentric is automatically more resilient" claim.
- The corpus's own Evidence Standards explicitly flag this as a claim the current evidence does **not** support — see [what the matrix does not support](/evidens/syntes/matris/).

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The evidence here is consistent with treating polycentric cooperation as a **genuine alternative architecture** for scale problems (see also R2), conditional on the cooperating units investing in relational and coordination infrastructure. It is not evidence that polycentric arrangements are automatically superior or automatically more resilient — that claim remains explicitly unsupported pending broader, more diverse cases.
