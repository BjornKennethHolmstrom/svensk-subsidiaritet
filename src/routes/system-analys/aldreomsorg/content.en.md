# Elderly care: continuity, relationships and the right level

## A system with great responsibility and known shortcomings

Elderly care is the responsibility of the municipalities under the Social Services Act, while the regions are responsible for the largest part of medical care. Care is provided both by the municipalities themselves and by private operators, who in most cases are funded by the municipality and work under the same rules and fees as municipal care. The vast majority of older people, even those over 80, remain in their own homes, often with the help of home care. Special housing is mainly for those with the greatest needs.

During 2025, shortcomings in home care and elderly care received media attention, and the Riksdag’s Committee on Social Affairs was informed by the government, the National Board of Health and Welfare and the Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions.

## Continuity: what older people value most

Research has long shown that those who receive home care value continuity of staff highly: receiving help from the same people, who know them and know what needs to be done. Nevertheless, in 2022 an older person with home care met an average of 16 different staff members over a two-week period. The number of home care recipients per care worker has also increased, from 9.16 on weekdays in 2024 to 10.06 in 2025.

Since July 2022, those who have home care must be offered a regular care contact, and since July 2023 the regular care contact must be an assistant nurse. The National Board of Health and Welfare’s follow-up shows that many have been offered a regular care contact, but that more should receive one and that the reform works better with certain providers than others. The Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions has stopped collecting statistics on staff continuity, and the government has tasked the National Board of Health and Welfare with developing a new measure.

## Current changes

- **New Social Services Act:** Since July 2025, a new Social Services Act with greater emphasis on preventive work has applied. The proposal for a separate Elderly Care Act was not implemented as a separate law, but parts of it have been incorporated into the Social Services Act.
- **Language requirements:** From July 2026, those who provide elderly care must work to ensure that staff have the Swedish language skills needed for the work. The government has proposed level B2 according to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages. The Committee on Social Affairs supported the proposal, with reservations from the Left Party and the Green Party.
- **Framework time:** Several municipalities, including Stockholm, are increasingly using so-called framework time, where the older person and staff together decide how the granted time is to be used, instead of each intervention being steered in detail.

## The cybernetic reading

The needs of older people vary greatly, and they change over time. Care that is governed by detailed decisions about each intervention can only handle a limited part of that variation. According to Ashby’s law, the gap can be met in two ways:

- **Strengthen capacity close to the older person:** regular care contact, framework time, continuity in the staff group and more room for staff judgement. Someone who knows the older person can adapt help to their daily condition.
- **Dampen variation through standards:** common requirements for competence, documentation and quality, which protect against neglect and provide equivalence between municipalities.

Both are needed. The question is the balance, and today much suggests that continuity and staff discretion are too weak.

Relationships outside care also matter. A frequently cited meta-analysis (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010) found that weak social relationships were linked to higher mortality. Care alone cannot replace such relationships, but it can make it easier or harder for older people to maintain them.

## Remaining problems

- **Continuity.** Many meet many different people, which makes security and quality more difficult.
- **Skills supply.** The number of older people is increasing, and it is becoming harder to recruit staff.
- **Cooperation between municipality and region.** Older people with major medical needs sometimes fall between care and healthcare.
- **Loneliness.** Many older people experience loneliness, especially those who live alone or have lost a partner.

## Subsidiarity-based proposals

The proposals are ideas to test in limited pilots with evaluation. They complement needs-assessed care and do not replace it.

### Small home care groups with framework time

Home care is organised in small, fixed groups responsible for a limited number of older people in an area, with framework time and great scope for the group to plan the work itself. The inspiration comes, among other things, from the Dutch Buurtzorg model in home healthcare, where small self-governing teams work close to patients. Evaluation: staff continuity, older people’s experience, staff working environment and costs.

### Neighbourhood networks

Voluntary networks where neighbours, associations and pensioners’ organisations help older people with company, walks and simpler errands, with the support of a coordinator in the municipality. The networks never replace needs-assessed interventions, and no one should lose help because they have neighbours who help.

### Cooperatives and idea-driven providers

Staff cooperatives and idea-driven organisations can already today be providers of home care and special housing, for example within freedom of choice systems under the Act on Freedom of Choice Systems (LOV) or through idea-driven public partnerships. The proposal is that municipalities facilitate such providers, for example through simpler requirements for small providers and support at start-up.

### Greater influence over interventions

The older person and the regular care contact are given greater opportunity to decide together how the granted time is used, within the framework of the assistance decision. This can be combined with a simplified needs assessment for smaller interventions.

### Intergenerational housing

Housing where older and younger people live in the same building, with shared spaces and activities. Such housing has been tested in Sweden, for example Sällbo in Helsingborg, where young and old live together. The proposal is that more municipal housing companies test similar models and evaluate them.

### Exchange of knowledge between generations

Opportunities for older people to contribute their knowledge, for example as homework helpers, mentors or in associations, in cooperation with schools, libraries and study associations.

## Objections

| Objection | How the proposals need to address it |
|---------|------|
| Informal help cannot replace professional care | Correct. The proposals complement, and the right to assistance is not affected. |
| Small providers may be vulnerable | Quality and supervision requirements apply to all providers, and the municipality is responsible if a provider cannot manage the task. |
| More self-governance for staff may lead to uneven quality | Clear goals, follow-up and supervision are needed. |
| Older people without relatives or networks are disadvantaged | Care must be designed so that it works even for those who lack a network. |

## Conclusion

Swedish elderly care has a large commitment and known shortcomings, especially in continuity and staff supply. The problems are not about care being professional, but about how it is organised: how many different people come into the home, how much room staff have to adapt help, and how well care cooperates with healthcare and with older people’s own networks. The proposals here are about strengthening capacity close to the older person, through small teams, framework time and more ways for civil society to contribute.

### Three things you can do

**If you are a municipal politician:** Find out how many different people an older person with home care meets in your municipality, and how many have a regular care contact.

**If you work in elderly care:** Investigate whether framework time is used, and what would be required to work in smaller, fixed groups.

**If you are a relative or neighbour:** Find out which pensioners’ organisations and associations offer visits and activities where the older person lives.
