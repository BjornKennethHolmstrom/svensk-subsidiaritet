# Transport: who plans what, and for whom?

## Responsibility is already divided across several levels

The transport system is governed at several levels at the same time:

- **The state** decides on the national plan for transport infrastructure. The Swedish Transport Administration prepares the proposal and the government makes the decision. The most recent plan covers 2026–2037.
- **The regions** decide on regional plans for infrastructure and are regional public transport authorities, with responsibility for bus services and regional rail services.
- **The municipalities** are responsible for streets, parking, cycle paths and physical planning, and can receive state co-financing for public transport and cycling through so-called urban environment agreements.

Earlier versions of this page described the Swedish Transport Administration as an authority with a planning monopoly. That is not correct: decisions on local and regional transport are to a large extent made by municipalities and regions.

## Some facts

- The car accounts for around 70 percent of passenger transport in Sweden, measured in passenger-kilometres.
- Railways account for a larger volume of passenger transport work than bus services. Earlier versions of this page claimed the opposite, which was wrong.
- Ostlänken is a roughly 160-kilometre-long new double-track railway between Järna and Linköping, with an estimated cost of SEK 102.8 billion in 2025 prices. The first trains are expected to run in 2035. The project’s socio-economic profitability is debated.
- The congestion tax in Gothenburg is part of the financing of the West Swedish Package. Of the package’s SEK 34 billion in 2009 prices, SEK 14 billion was to come from the congestion tax. The package’s largest project is Västlänken, a railway tunnel under Gothenburg. The package also includes public transport, a new bridge and a road tunnel. Earlier versions of this page claimed that 80 percent of the revenue goes to road capacity, which was wrong.
- The state electric car subsidy was abolished in November 2022.

## Real tensions

- **City and countryside.** Public transport is expensive to operate where few people live, and in sparsely populated areas many depend on the car. At the same time, that is where distances to services are longest.
- **Socio-economic calculations.** National investments are prioritised partly with the help of socio-economic calculations, in which travel time savings weigh heavily. How the calculations should be used, and what they capture and do not capture, is debated.
- **Large projects and everyday journeys.** Large railway projects tie up a great deal of money over a long time, while a large share of journeys are short and local.
- **Children’s journeys.** The proportion of children who walk or cycle to school has decreased. How much, and why, varies between studies.
- **Parking and land use.** Municipalities decide on parking, and how much land is used for parking affects both costs and travel habits.

## The cybernetic reading

Travel needs vary greatly: by age, place, income, ability and time of day. No single planner can oversee everything. But different parts of the transport system require different levels:

- **National:** main lines, freight corridors, major roads and common rules, where scale and coordination over long distances are crucial.
- **Regional:** public transport and regional infrastructure, because labour markets and travel patterns extend across municipal boundaries.
- **Local:** streets, cycle paths, parking, school routes and small solutions such as demand-responsive transport and car pools, where local knowledge is crucial.

Subsidiarity here is not about taking responsibility away from the state, but about ensuring that each level has the tools and resources for its part.

## Subsidiarity-based proposals

The proposals are ideas to test in limited pilots with evaluation.

### Shared mobility in residential areas

Housing companies, tenant-owner associations or associations of residents receive start-up support for shared electric bicycles, cargo bikes and car pools. Municipalities can link this to lower requirements for parking spaces in new construction, so-called flexible parking norms. Evaluation: use, car ownership and costs for households.

### Safe school routes

Municipalities map school routes together with pupils and parents and address dangerous crossings. Parents can organise so-called walking school buses. School transport is not affected: it is a right for pupils with long distances, disabilities or traffic-dangerous school routes, and should not be confused with parents driving their children.

### Flexible public transport in rural areas

Regions and municipalities test demand-responsive transport, coordination of school transport, transport services for people with disabilities and scheduled services, as well as car-sharing supported by digital services. Evaluation: accessibility for those without a car, cost per journey and satisfaction.

### Greater local influence over regional public transport

Municipalities and residents gain a clearer role in how regional public transport is planned in their area, for example through local transport councils with which the region consults before traffic changes.

### Mobility hubs

Some of the parking areas in urban areas are converted into places with charging, car pools, bicycle parking, rental and parcel lockers.

## Objections

| Objection | How the proposals need to address it |
|---------|------|
| The car is needed in rural areas | Yes. The proposals aim to provide more alternatives, not to remove the car. |
| Shared services only work in urban areas | That is why different solutions are proposed for different places, and evaluation of what works where. |
| Local solutions can become fragmented | Regional coordination of tickets, information and service patterns is needed. |
| Large infrastructure projects are also needed | Correct. The proposals concern everyday journeys, not replacing national infrastructure. |

## Conclusion

Swedish transport policy is not a centrally planned whole, but a system in which the state, regions and municipalities have different roles. The tensions lie rather in how resources are distributed between large projects and everyday journeys, between city and countryside, and in how well the different levels cooperate. The proposals here concern strengthening the local and regional tools for everyday journeys.

### Three things you can do

**If you are a municipal politician:** Find out whether the municipality has flexible parking norms and whether it has applied for urban environment agreements.

**If you are a parent:** Map the school route together with other parents and the school, and propose measures for dangerous crossings.

**If you live in a rural area:** Find out what opportunities for demand-responsive transport exist in your region.
