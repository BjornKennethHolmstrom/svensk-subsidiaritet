# Welfare Rings

[← Back to the main article](..)

**Summary:** Networks of 10–20 households in the same area that help each other with everyday things, such as rides, childcare, meals and company, and that keep in contact with one another so that someone who begins to become isolated or has difficulties is noticed early. The rings are linked to a coordinator in the municipality who can refer people onward to social services or healthcare when needs become greater.

*This is a proposal to test in limited pilots with evaluation. The figures below are proposals and rough estimates, not results.*

## Why

Many needs are only discovered once they have become large: when someone can no longer manage their finances, their health or their loneliness and turns to healthcare or social services. Neighbours and friends often see the signs earlier, but lack a natural way to act. The new Social Services Act emphasises preventive and early interventions, and that opens the way for the municipality to support such networks.

## How it would work

A Welfare Ring is a voluntary network that:

- meets regularly, for example once a month
- shares information about who can help with what, and who needs help
- offers simple everyday help, without requiring anything in return
- keeps in contact, so that someone who has not been heard from for a while gets a call
- knows where to turn when the need is greater than the ring can handle, for example in the case of serious illness, domestic violence or mental crisis

**Support from the municipality:**
- a coordinator who helps the rings get started, answers questions and maintains contact with social services and healthcare
- a small start-up grant for room rent and materials
- as a possible option: a small remuneration to active participants, for example in the form of a tax deduction

## Pilot proposal

**Scope:** Around one hundred rings in five to ten municipalities over three years, in different types of areas: rural areas, suburbs and areas with many older people living alone.

**Evaluation:** compare areas with and without rings with regard to self-rated loneliness, having someone to ask for help, early contacts with social services and how participants and other residents experience the rings. The evaluation should also examine who participates and who does not.

| Start-up grant | 100 rings × SEK 2,000 | SEK 0.2 million |
| Remuneration to participants (if included) | 100 rings × 15 households × SEK 500/month | approx. SEK 9 million |
| Evaluation | | approx. SEK 2 million |
| **Total** | | **approx. SEK 14 million** |

Whether the rings should be expanded after the pilot depends on what the evaluation shows. Remuneration to participants is the largest item and the most uncertain part of the proposal.

## Legal issues to investigate

1. **Remuneration:** A tax deduction or other remuneration to participants requires legislative change and a register of the rings. It should be tested whether remuneration adds anything, or whether coordinators and start-up grants are sufficient.
2. **Personal data:** Information about neighbours’ health and situation is sensitive. The Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act applies to authorities, not private networks, but data protection rules also apply to private individuals in organised contexts. The rings need simple rules about what is shared and with whom, and information about health and finances should only be shared with the consent of the person concerned.

## Objections

**“This is surveilling neighbours.”** The rings must be based on voluntariness and respect for those who do not want to participate. Someone not participating must never in itself become a signal that something is wrong.

**“Those who need the most help have the least network.”** That is a real risk. Informal help tends to reach those who already have contacts. The rings should therefore be a complement to public interventions, and the right to support from social services must not depend on whether one has a ring.

**“Fraud with the remuneration.”** If remuneration is included, simple checks are needed. That is also a reason to test pilots both with and without remuneration.

**“It replaces professional interventions.”** The purpose is the opposite: to catch needs early and refer people onward, not to replace healthcare or social services.

## Experience to build on

- **Neighbourhood watch**, which shows that neighbourhood networks can be organised in Swedish residential areas.
- **Voluntary organisations** such as the Red Cross, the Church of Sweden and others already run visiting activities and support for people who are lonely.
- **Village councils and community hall associations** in rural areas, with a long tradition of local cooperation.

## What you can do

**If you are a municipal politician:** Propose a small pilot with a coordinator who supports a few rings in one area, and a plan for evaluation.

**If you are a housing company or property owner:** Offer premises where neighbours can meet regularly.

**If you are a resident:** Invite your neighbours and talk about how you can help each other, without requiring anything of anyone.
