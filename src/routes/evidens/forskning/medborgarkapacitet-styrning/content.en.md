# Civic Capability & Governance

## Which knowledge, skills, social capabilities and forms of participation are required for decentralized and adaptive governance to work well — and to what extent can institutions themselves develop these capabilities?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Exploratory
**Confidence:** Low
**Key finding:** International literature gives some support to the idea that meaningful participation can itself build civic knowledge and political efficacy — but the deeper claim that governance architecture and human capability co-evolve is currently a conceptual framework, not an empirically tested one.
**Explicit caution:** This is the most exploratory topic in the corpus. It should not be used to justify claims about "developmental stages," "consciousness," or similar constructs — see below for why.

---

## The question

Decentralized and participatory governance assumes people can actually use the authority and information they're given. This topic asks the question directly rather than assuming the answer:

> What civic, cognitive and social capabilities does decentralized, adaptive governance actually require — and can institutions themselves help build those capabilities, creating a feedback loop rather than a one-directional dependency?

---

## Four distinct constructs — kept deliberately separate

- **Civic knowledge** — understanding institutions, rights, responsibilities, decision processes. Relatively easy to measure.
- **Civic skills** — deliberation, argumentation, listening, evidence evaluation, cooperation, conflict resolution. Reasonably well-studied.
- **Systems reasoning** — understanding feedback, time delays, indirect effects, trade-offs, complex causal chains. Much more specific to this project's needs, and considerably less established as a measurable construct.
- **Epistemic/developmental orientation** — worldview, values, "developmental stage" frameworks such as Spiral Dynamics. **We treat this with the most caution of the four** — see below.

---

## What the evidence shows

OECD's civic-education synthesis connects civic education to political and social engagement, and finds civic education can strengthen knowledge and participation — though causality and effect size vary. A 2025 meta-analysis of roughly 100 quantitative studies of citizen-oriented mini-publics found participation improved participants' political knowledge, political attitudes, internal political efficacy, and reasoning ability; effects on broader civic engagement were considerably less certain.

This gives partial, international support to two hypotheses:

**H17** (participation can itself improve civic knowledge, efficacy and deliberative capability) has some genuine initial support from this literature.

**H16** (decentralized decision-making works better when actors have sufficient civic/institutional capability) is conceptually well-motivated but has essentially no dedicated empirical corpus yet — Swedish case work (S2's finding that smaller municipalities used their new formal autonomy only weakly for years) is *suggestive* of a capacity/capability gap, but S2's capacity problem is administrative and organizational, not necessarily "civic capability" in the sense this topic means.

---

## Why we're cautious about "systems reasoning" and developmental frameworks

Systems reasoning is plausible and directly relevant to Governance as Engineering's own way of thinking, but it currently lacks a validated, independent measurement instrument in this corpus. There's a specific methodological risk worth naming explicitly: if "systems reasoning" or a "higher developmental stage" is defined by the very traits the project expects to correlate with good governance outcomes, any correlation found would be close to circular. We therefore explicitly do **not** adopt Spiral Dynamics or similar developmental-stage frameworks as a scientific basis for this research programme. They may exist elsewhere in the broader intellectual context around this project, but the Evidence corpus's H20 is deliberately marked exploratory/low-confidence, and H19's biggest need is a robust, independently validated measure — evaluated on its own merits before being adopted for anything.

## A useful reframing this topic motivates

Rather than "problem → governance architecture," the more defensible model separates problem structure from the human system operating within it:

```text
              PROBLEM
                 │
       ┌─────────┴─────────┐
       ▼                   ▼
Problem structure      Human system
       │                   │
       ▼                   ▼
Governance fit       Capability fit
       │                   │
       └─────────┬─────────┘
                 ▼
            GOVERNANCE
             OUTCOME
                 │
                 ▼
              FEEDBACK
              ↙      ↘
       institutions  people
              ↘      ↙
            adaptation
```

This reframes governance effectiveness as potentially depending on *both* architecture and actor capability — conceptually close to "governance effectiveness = architecture × capability" — but this remains an unvalidated conceptual model (H20), not an empirical finding.

---

## Counterevidence / limits

- The corpus currently has no dedicated Swedish case testing civic-capability development directly (e.g., a citizens'-assembly or deliberative-participation case with before/after measures).
- International mini-public findings on broader civic engagement are considerably weaker and less durable than the knowledge/efficacy findings — this topic should not overstate what the literature supports.
- The risk of circular measurement is real and should be treated as a standing methodological constraint on this topic, not a solved problem.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

R8 should be read as an **active research direction, not an established finding**. Its most defensible current claim is narrow: some forms of structured citizen participation appear to build civic knowledge and political efficacy in international evidence. Everything beyond that — institutional co-evolution, systems reasoning as a governance variable, and especially developmental-stage frameworks — remains exploratory and should be labeled as such wherever it's referenced elsewhere on this site.
