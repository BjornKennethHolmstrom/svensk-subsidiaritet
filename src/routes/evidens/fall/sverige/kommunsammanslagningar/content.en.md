# S1 — Swedish Municipal Mergers, 1952–1974

**Country:** Sweden · **Period:** 1952–1974 (with a 1977 final adjustment) · **Domain:** Territorial/administrative reform · **Status:** Finished draft, initial Swedish case set

---

## Case at a glance

Sweden merged its municipalities twice in twenty years: from 2,498 municipalities in 1951, to 1,037 after the 1952 reform, to 278 by 1974 (277 after a final 1977 adjustment). The reform was justified as necessary to give municipalities enough administrative and financial capacity to handle expanding public responsibilities — and, explicitly, as a way to *preserve* municipal self-government against further centralization to the state. It simultaneously produced one of the largest reductions in the number of independent local political units in Swedish history.

**What actually changed?** Municipal *capacity* increased by most accounts. Municipal *democracy* became measurably more distant: parliamentary material from the period itself reports a fall in elected local representatives from roughly 225,000 (1952) to about 42,000 (1974), and links this directly to increased distance between citizens and municipal government. Whether decision-making moved closer to or further from the people it affected is not a single answer — it depends on which function, and on which part of the merged municipality, you ask about.

---

## Context

By the mid-20th century, Swedish municipalities' responsibilities had grown both more numerous and more technically demanding. The 1946 government proposition (Prop. 1946:236) describes small municipalities as often lacking the economic and administrative capacity to meet these demands, and as struggling to recruit enough qualified elected officials.

Importantly, the proposition itself lists several alternatives that were considered and rejected: increased voluntary municipal cooperation, moving tasks up to the counties or the state, higher state grants, or — the option chosen — creating larger, more viable municipalities. Several consultation bodies explicitly saw strong municipal self-government as a counterweight to the ongoing centralization of public administration, and the proposition warns that moving tasks to the state or counties could *undermine* municipal self-rule.

This reframes the reform's own self-understanding: it was conceived less as "centralization is better" and more as **"if we don't make the local level viable enough, functions will end up centralized anyway."**

---

## What was intended?

The core argument was functional: larger municipalities would have the administrative and financial capacity to manage increasingly demanding public responsibilities and coordinate services efficiently, while remaining self-governing relative to the state.

The 1946 committee proposed roughly 3,000–4,000 inhabitants as a target size, explicitly cautioning against a purely numerical criterion — geography, transport links, history and local conditions were meant to matter too. By 1961, a second proposition (Prop. 1961:180) set a new guideline of around 8,000 inhabitants by 1975, organized around the principle of economically coherent regions built around viable central places ("centralorter") rather than population alone.

## What changed formally?

- Municipal territorial boundaries were consolidated in two waves (1952 and 1962–1974, with a final adjustment in 1977).
- The 1971 structural reform removed the former legal distinction between cities, market towns and rural municipalities, standardizing a single primary-municipality structure.
- The formal decision chain ran: Riksdag (legal framework) → County Board (investigation and proposals) → municipalities (consultation only) → the Chamber of Justice (Kammarkollegiet) → the Government, which finalized new municipal boundaries. This was not a bottom-up process.
- Crucially, the *method* changed partway through: the 1952 reform and the initial phase of the 1962 reform were based on voluntary municipal participation. In 1969, the Riksdag decided the "kommunblock" reform would be completed through mergers **regardless of municipal consent**, targeting completion by 1973/74. A 1969 constitutional-committee statement explicitly records objections to abandoning the voluntariness principle.

## What changed materially?

The consolidation clearly reduced the number of political units and elected officials. Contemporary parliamentary material reports the fall from roughly 225,000 to about 42,000 municipal elected representatives between 1952 and 1974, explicitly linking this to increased distance between citizens and municipal government.

However, the consequences were not uniform within merged municipalities. A geocoded difference-in-differences study (Harjunen, Saarimaa & Tukiainen) found **no overall effect on municipal expenditure after eight years** — directly challenging the assumption that "larger municipalities are automatically cheaper" — but did find **highly unequal geographic representation** in post-merger councils. Smaller, politically marginalized former municipalities experienced substantial reductions in local public-sector employment in administration and health/social care, and house-price evidence suggested a deterioration in the local service-tax bundle in those specific areas.

A separate study (Hanes, 2014) found that the 1952 reform had a **negative** effect on expenditure specifically when municipalities remained below a critical size afterward — again complicating any simple "bigger is cheaper" story.

---

## Authority topology

**Before:** many relatively small municipalities, each with its own elected council and administration.

**After:** decision authority consolidated at the level of far fewer, larger municipalities. The formal decision chain for the reform itself ran top-down (Riksdag → County Board → Kammarkollegiet → Government), even though the stated goal was to strengthen municipal self-government relative to the state.

**Our read:** this is a genuine increase in *territorial* centralization — even though the resulting larger municipalities remained formally self-governing relative to the state. The reform is best understood as **territorial centralization undertaken to enable functional/vertical decentralization** — a more interesting and more precise description than a simple centralization-vs-decentralization framing.

## Information topology

The 1961 guidelines deliberately tried to follow actual economic and social geography — "economically coherent regions" built around real commuting and service patterns, not just existing administrative lines. That's an early, concrete example of what we now call **functional scale / boundary fit**.

At the same time, the information *process* ran: local reality → local consultation (yttrande) → County Board planning → state ratification. Local information could enter the process, but the decision architecture aggregated it upward rather than acting on it locally. How much local variation survived that representation chain is not yet quantified in our corpus — but the potential loss points are now identifiable, which is itself useful.

This is resource pooling through territorial fusion — directly relevant to Hypothesis H9 on equalization.

## Initiative and discretion

Initiative likely increased at the new, larger municipal level relative to the small municipalities it replaced, but this has not yet been reconstructed at the level of specific functions.

## Capacity

Capacity-building was the reform's principal stated rationale, and there is real support for it: SCB's figures show average population per municipality rising from 1,583 to 4,004 after the 1952 reform, and small, sub-1,000-inhabitant municipalities falling from 1,226 to just three. But the expenditure evidence does not support a simple claim that this produced aggregate fiscal savings — see Hanes (2014) above.

## Coordination

Fewer, larger municipalities reduced the *number* of independent local actors requiring coordination, but created larger *internal* coordination challenges — and did not automatically preserve the political voice of all constituent areas within the new, larger unit.

## Nested centralization

**Assessment: a genuine open question, not a settled "yes."**

Vertically, relative to the state: **not primarily** — an explicit goal was to *avoid* having to move municipal tasks up to the state or counties, in order to preserve local self-government.

Horizontally, between the former local units: **clearly yes** — 2,498 → 1,037 → 278 represents an enormous concentration of local political authority into far fewer decision centres.

Internally, within the new municipality: **likely to some degree, but requires further empirical reconstruction.** We have strong evidence of fewer elected positions and greater geographic distance, but that doesn't automatically tell us how much actual decision-making power moved from, say, constituent sub-areas to central municipal administration.

The more precise formulation is therefore: **territorial consolidation increased the scale at which municipal authority was exercised, while its effects on decision-making within the new municipality require separate reconstruction** — not the blunter claim "S1 shows nested centralization."

---

## Observed outcomes

**Administrative / fiscal:** no overall expenditure effect after eight years (Harjunen, Saarimaa & Tukiainen); earlier work (Hanes 2014) suggests scale effects are non-linear and size-dependent, not uniformly positive.

**Democratic / representational:** strong geographic inequality in political representation after merger; smaller, less-represented former municipalities lost local public-sector jobs relative to better-represented areas; contemporary political material explicitly raised concerns about increased distance between citizens and local government.

**Political process:** historical research (Erlingsson, Wångmar & Ödalen) argues the reforms were not simply consensual, rational adaptations to changing conditions — distributional conflict and the 1969 shift from voluntary to compulsory implementation mattered to how the reform actually played out.

## The self-correction: local organs (1979)

Perhaps the most theoretically interesting part of this case. In 1979, new legislation (SFS 1979:408) allowed municipal councils to establish local organs — district councils, institutional boards, and similar bodies — including, on a trial basis, for specialized functions like social services, healthcare, schools, planning and traffic.

This looks like a **second-order correction**: territorial consolidation raised capacity and coordination but reduced democratic proximity; local organs then attempted to bring decision-making and influence back closer to citizens. But critically, the municipal council retained control over the local organs' remit and geographic area, and a local organ could only refer matters to the council if the council had specifically authorized this. That's a concrete example of **partial re-decentralization within a larger, already-decentralized unit** — good evidence that decentralization is not a single level-question; it is recursive.

---

## Counterevidence / qualifications

- Larger municipalities can provide capacity genuinely unavailable to very small ones — the case doesn't support "small is always better" any more than it supports "big is always better."
- The merger reforms had real functional motivations, documented in the primary sources, not only political ones.
- Some expenditure benefits may appear at particular sizes or for particular functions even where aggregate savings are weak or absent.
- Democratic effects need to be kept analytically distinct from administrative effectiveness — they don't necessarily move together, and this case shows them moving in different directions simultaneously.

## Alternative explanations

- Urbanization and demographic change were happening simultaneously and independently of the merger policy.
- Welfare-state expansion changed municipal responsibilities on its own timeline, independent of the mergers themselves.
- Central-place economic geography may explain some outcomes better than jurisdiction size per se.
- Political representation patterns may mediate service changes rather than population size directly causing them.

---

## What this case teaches us

S1 motivated an addition to our whole evidence model: **"decentralization" without a specified direction is too blunt a concept.** This case shows at least four distinct directions operating at once:

- **Vertical** (state ↔ municipality): the municipal level was strengthened relative to the state.
- **Horizontal** (small municipality ↔ larger municipality): the number of municipal decision centres fell drastically.
- **Internal** (municipality ↔ sub-municipal area/institution): later problems with local anchoring motivated the 1979 local-organs reform.
- **Functional** (one institution/domain ↔ another): different municipal functions had different optimal scales even in the state's own 1961 reasoning — building regulation could sometimes work better in small, sparsely populated municipalities, fire services had no clear optimal size, while other functions needed a larger population base.

That last point is close to a historical precedent for exactly the "functional scale fit" hypothesis (H7, H14) this project is trying to formalize: there was no single optimal scale, only a series of function-specific trade-offs that the state itself explicitly recognized in the 1961 proposition as a tension between municipal efficiency and municipal democracy.
