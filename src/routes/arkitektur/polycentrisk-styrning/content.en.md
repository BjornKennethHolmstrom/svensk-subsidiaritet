# Polycentric Governance

## Many independent but coordinated decision centres

At a certain point in the spectrum, we stop assuming there must be **one** governance architecture for the entire state. Instead, governance becomes partly issue-specific.

Energy might involve municipalities, regions, a national regulator, grid operators, citizens and energy communities. Water might have another architecture. Healthcare a third. Biodiversity a fourth. Digital infrastructure a fifth.

This is genuinely polycentric governance: multiple relatively independent but interdependent decision centres interacting within a shared institutional architecture.

---

## Polycentric is not the same as chaotic

This is an important distinction. Polycentric governance requires rules governing the **relationships between centres**. The system isn't "everyone does whatever they want" — it's "multiple autonomous centres coordinate within a shared constitutional architecture."

---

## From domain to nesting

**Domain-polycentric governance** lets different policy domains have partly autonomous, coordinating ecosystems, rather than forcing one governance pattern to fit every sector of society.

**Deep subsidiarity** adds a strong vertical principle: for each function, we ask *at what scale does this problem actually exist?* — rather than assuming national → regional → local is always the right hierarchy. An ecological problem, for example, might naturally require watershed or bioregional governance rather than municipal or national boundaries.

**Nested polycentric governance** combines polycentricity, subsidiarity and nesting:

```
GLOBAL
  ↕
Regional / federated
  ↕
Bioregional
  ↕
National
  ↕
Regional
  ↕
Municipal
  ↕
Community
  ↕
Individual
```

But this is **not** a conventional hierarchy. Authority doesn't simply flow downward. Information flows upward, downward and sideways, and institutions interact both vertically and horizontally.

**GGF-style plural governance** adds one more dimension: legitimate knowledge and legitimate interests don't come only from voters, politicians and agencies, but also from local communities, Indigenous governance, science, ecological "guardians," civil society and future generations. The point is not "everyone gets a vote" — it is:

> **match governance authority to the kind of knowledge, affectedness and responsibility relevant to the decision.**

---

## Authority that escalates and de-escalates

In its most developed form, the **adaptive polycentric commonwealth**, authority can move dynamically.

A local ecological problem stays local as long as it stays local. If monitoring shows the effects spreading across several municipalities, coordination moves up to the regional level. If effects become national, it moves further. If they become transboundary, it moves to the international level — and can move back down again if conditions change.

That is closer to the engineering concept of a **dynamic control architecture** than a traditional administrative hierarchy, and it requires the escalation and de-escalation rules to be clearly defined in advance, not improvised in the moment.
