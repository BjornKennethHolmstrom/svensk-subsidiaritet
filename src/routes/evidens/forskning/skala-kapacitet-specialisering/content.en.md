# Scale, Capacity & Specialization

## When does larger scale provide superior administrative capacity, efficiency or specialization?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported
**Confidence:** Medium
**Key finding:** Scale can genuinely increase capacity and enable specialization, but larger scale does not reliably reduce total costs — and the same capacity gains are sometimes achievable without merging jurisdictions at all.
**Key caveat:** Do not conflate this topic with R1 — local information advantage and scale advantage are different mechanisms that can point in opposite directions for the same reform.

---

## The question

"Bigger is more efficient" and "bigger is cheaper" are routinely treated as the same claim. They aren't. This topic separates them:

> Under what conditions does a larger governance unit have genuinely superior capacity, and under what conditions does that capacity advantage translate into better outcomes or lower costs?

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Administrative capacity** — legal, analytical, and organizational ability to plan, implement and evaluate policy.
- **Specialization** — access to professional or technical competence too costly for a small unit to maintain alone.
- **Economies of scale** — declining average cost per unit as production scale increases, which does not hold uniformly across all governance functions.
- **Diseconomies of scale** — coordination costs, loss of local information, and reduced political voice that can grow with size.
- **Factorizability** — the possibility that capacity/specialization gains can be obtained through mechanisms other than merging political jurisdictions (shared financing, shared functions, cooperation).

---

## What the evidence shows

The clearest capacity evidence in the corpus comes from **[municipal mergers (S1)](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/)**: average population per municipality rose from 1,583 to 4,004 after the 1952 reform, and municipalities under 1,000 inhabitants fell from 1,226 to just three — a genuine capacity increase by any administrative measure. But the same case's expenditure evidence directly contradicts a simple "bigger is cheaper" story: a geocoded difference-in-differences study found no overall effect on municipal expenditure after eight years, and a separate study found the reform had a *negative* effect on expenditure specifically for municipalities that remained below a critical size afterward.

**[Intermunicipal cooperation (S5)](/evidens/fall/sverige/mellankommunal-samverkan/)** provides the sharpest evidence that capacity and specialization gains do not require merger at all: municipalities cooperate heavily in specialized, technical or regulatory functions (environmental enforcement, emergency services, procurement, IT, legal competence) while rarely cooperating in large, continuous, citizen-facing functions (primary school, elderly care). And where S5 does show cost effects, they run against the "scale saves money" intuition too: cooperation tends to improve capacity, specialization and resilience rather than reduce costs — the mechanism looks more like *slower future cost growth from better capacity* than *immediate savings*.

**[Fiscal equalization (S4)](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunalekonomisk-utjamning/)** shows a third path: pooling *financial capacity* to compensate for structural cost differences (age structure, geography, density) without pooling *decision-making* at all. This directly supports the idea that scale-related benefits can sometimes be "factorized" — obtained through a mechanism other than merging the underlying political units.

---

## Where findings agree

- Larger scale can produce genuine, measurable increases in administrative and specialist capacity.
- Capacity gains do not automatically produce cost savings — these are separate outcomes and should be measured separately.
- Different governance functions have different scale requirements; a single "optimal municipality size" is not well supported by the evidence.

## Where findings diverge

- Whether merger or cooperation is the better mechanism for achieving capacity gains depends heavily on the specific function — the corpus doesn't yet support a general ranking.
- Cost effects of scale appear non-linear and size-dependent (per S1's Hanes 2014 finding) rather than uniformly positive or negative.

---

## Counterevidence

- Direct evidence against "larger is cheaper": no aggregate expenditure effect from Sweden's largest-ever municipal consolidation (S1), and a documented negative effect for municipalities that remained small after merging.
- Cooperation (S5) achieving specialization gains without merger directly challenges the assumption that scale benefits require jurisdictional consolidation.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The evidence in this corpus is most consistent with claim **[C4 — scale can be factorized](/evidens/syntes/vad-vi-vet/)**: some capacity and specialization benefits associated with scale can potentially be obtained through cooperation or shared financing without merging political jurisdictions. This remains "promising," not "established" — a direct, controlled comparison of merger vs. cooperation vs. independent provision for the same function is still missing from the corpus (see [Open Questions](/evidens/syntes/oppna-fragor/)).
