# Counterevidence

## Not "the opposition" — what makes our hypotheses less credible

This page collects evidence that challenges subsidiarity assumptions, decentralization assumptions, GAE-derived interpretations, proposed mechanisms, or current synthesis claims. We treat this as a first-class page, not a caveat buried at the bottom of an article, because a research programme that doesn't foreground what challenges it isn't doing research.

---

## Against a general decentralization preference

**Expenditure evidence.** A geocoded difference-in-differences study of Sweden's municipal mergers (Harjunen, Saarimaa & Tukiainen) found **no overall effect on municipal expenditure after eight years** — directly against the idea that consolidation reliably saves money. A separate study (Hanes 2014) found the 1952 merger reform had a **negative** effect on expenditure specifically for municipalities that remained below a critical size afterward. Together these directly contradict "larger is always cheaper," but they equally fail to support "smaller is always cheaper" — see [Case S1](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/).

**Representation costs.** The same merger reforms produced measurable, unequal geographic representation within merged municipalities, with smaller, less-represented former municipalities losing local public-sector jobs relative to better-represented areas. This is direct evidence *against* treating consolidation as a free capacity gain.

## Against a general capacity-building narrative

**Nested centralization risk.** Sweden's school decentralization (S2) was intended to devolve authority below the municipal level, but evidence shows uneven local steering — formal decentralization without a corresponding shift in practical authority, information or initiative (hypothesis H4). This directly challenges any assumption that formal reform reliably produces material change.

## Against cooperation as a costless alternative to merger

**Coordination and accountability costs.** Evidence on intermunicipal cooperation (S5) shows real transaction costs, ongoing coordination burden, and — depending on institutional form — reduced individual municipal accountability and transparency. Cooperation is not a free substitute for merger or centralization; it trades one set of costs for another.

## Against assuming preference satisfaction equals welfare

Hypothesis H2 (preference heterogeneity increases the value of local differentiation) is theoretically well-motivated but has a specific vulnerability: giving a locality what it prefers is not the same as improving welfare. Rights, spillovers, and scale effects can dominate preference satisfaction, and our current Swedish case set doesn't yet test this distinction directly.

## Against uncritical use of GAE formal results

Several GAE-derived hypotheses (H11, H12, H13, H15) currently have only **partial or low mechanism fidelity** against real institutional evidence — see the **[Evidence Matrix](/evidens/syntes/matris/)**. GAE X's empirical test of observer-diversity effects, for instance, is domain-specific (an AI test) and has not been shown to generalize to governance institutions. Treating these as settled institutional facts rather than [R]-level model results would be a mistake we're explicitly trying to avoid — see **[How We Know](/evidens/sa-vet-vi/)**.

## What would count as stronger counterevidence

We would treat the following, if found, as significant challenges to the project's current working hypotheses:

- Consistent evidence that local decision-making does **not** improve information use even when autonomy and capacity are both high.
- Systematic evidence that intermunicipal cooperation performs **worse** than both merger and independent provision across multiple functions, not just higher coordination cost but worse outcomes.
- Evidence that citizen participation produces **no** measurable capability gains even in well-designed deliberative processes (currently H17 has some initial supporting evidence — a strong null result here would be important).
- Cases where functional scale-matching does **not** correlate with better outcomes, undermining C3.

## A methodological note

We deliberately avoid classifying cases as simply "pro-subsidiarity" or "anti-subsidiarity." We use: **local advantage / central advantage / hybrid advantage / unclear.** That's more informative and less ideologically loaded — and it's why, for example, [Case S1](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/) is not filed as either a subsidiarity success or failure, but as a case with genuinely mixed and multidimensional evidence.
