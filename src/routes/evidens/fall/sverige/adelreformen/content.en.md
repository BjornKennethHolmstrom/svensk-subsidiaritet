# S3 — Ädelreformen, 1992

**Country:** Sweden · **Primary period:** 1990–2004, with later consequences · **Domain:** Elder care, healthcare, municipal governance, inter-organizational coordination · **Status:** Finished draft

---

## Case at a glance

The 1992 Ädelreformen transferred most long-term institutional elder care, and important parts of healthcare, from county councils to municipalities — aiming for clearer responsibility and a more integrated response to older people's social and medical needs. The reform produced substantial structural and quality improvements, but deliberately retained physicians and parts of primary healthcare at the county level, leaving persistent coordination problems precisely for people whose needs crossed that boundary.

**What actually changed?** Municipalities gained coherent responsibility for nearly 40,000 care places and roughly 55,000 employees. What didn't change: physicians stayed in the county system. That single retained boundary is the architectural key to the whole case.

---

## Context

Ädelreformen addressed a long-standing problem of **fragmented responsibility** between municipalities and county councils — a problem discussed since at least the 1950s. It was especially acute for older people needing several kinds of assistance simultaneously: social care, home help, residential care, primary healthcare, hospital care, rehabilitation, assistive devices, and physicians — functions that did not all belong to the same principal. The reform is a clean example of a Governance Fit problem: **the person's functional need did not correspond to the institutional boundaries through which the system was organized.**

## What was intended?

**Create clearer responsibility** — municipalities would get coherent responsibility for long-term services and care for older and disabled people.

**Create a more integrated, holistic view of the older person**, meeting social and medical needs in a coordinated way.

**Reduce institutionalization** — a deliberate departure from a hospital-oriented long-term-care model, strengthening the right to remain in one's ordinary home or a more home-like special housing form.

**Enable structural transformation** — municipal responsibility was meant to make it easier to convert traditional nursing homes into special housing with integrated care and support.

## What changed formally?

From 1 January 1992, municipalities assumed overarching responsibility for healthcare in special forms of housing and day activities, major parts of long-term institutional care, financial responsibility for medically ready-for-discharge patients under specified conditions, and responsibility for special housing. They also gained the legal ability to take over home healthcare in ordinary housing under specified conditions. The later government review characterizes the transfer as covering **nearly 40,000 care places and approximately 55,000 employees**, at a cost of more than SEK 20 billion.

## What did *not* change

The single most important architectural fact: municipal healthcare responsibility **did not include physicians**. The 1990/91 proposition deliberately kept physicians within the county healthcare system, reasoning that physician activity should remain integrated within primary healthcare. So the resulting architecture wasn't a single principal split into social care and healthcare — it was two separate principals (municipality and county council) meeting at the level of a shared patient:

```text
                    INDIVIDUAL
                        │
              ┌─────────┴─────────┐
              ▼                   ▼
          MUNICIPALITY        COUNTY COUNCIL
              │                   │
       social care           healthcare
       nursing etc.          physicians
       special housing       primary care
              │                   │
              └─────────┬─────────┘
                        │
                   shared patient
```

## Authority topology

The reform created what's best described as a **boundary architecture**. The question is not simply "which level has authority?" but **can the system maintain a coherent care trajectory when relevant authority is split across levels?**

## Resource topology

The transfer came with substantial fiscal restructuring: tax switching between county councils and municipalities, changes to fiscal equalization, a new state grant for municipal services and care, an intermunicipal cost-equalization mechanism, and special restructuring support. This was an explicit **resource transfer architecture**, not "here's a new responsibility, find the money yourself" — directly relevant to H3.

A particularly interesting incentive mechanism: the reform introduced municipal financial responsibility for certain patients who remained in hospital after being medically ready for discharge, explicitly intended to create incentives for municipalities and hospitals to resolve bottlenecks and free hospital capacity. A classic governance-engineering move (observed bottleneck → change financial responsibility → change incentives → change behavior → reduce bottleneck) — but one that also creates a risk: each principal may optimize its own budget position, which is exactly why the joint system needs to be examined, not just the individual actors.

## Information topology

The problem was explicitly an individual-level integration problem: information required for a coherent decision was distributed across different institutional systems. A municipal nurse could hold critical information about a person's everyday condition while the physician belonged organizationally to the county system. This is an unusually clear case of **distributed information + divided authority**. Problems with information transfer appeared early enough that Socialstyrelsen issued 1996 regulations specifically on information transfer and coordinated care planning at hospital discharge — evidence that the problem wasn't just "the wrong institution has responsibility," but that **information wasn't reliably crossing the institutional boundary at the moment responsibility changed:** a signal-fidelity / boundary-permeability problem.

## Capacity topology

The transfer moved large numbers of staff, beds and responsibilities to municipalities, but organizational responsibility didn't automatically transfer every relevant capability — physicians stayed in county healthcare. The 2003 review identified insufficient medical competence in municipal care, inadequate physician participation, weak continuity, and rehabilitation deficiencies. The problem wasn't simply a lack of local capacity — it was **capacity distributed across incompatible organizational boundaries.**

## Coordination topology

Probably the strongest dimension of this case. The 2003 government review explicitly found that, despite positive developments, significant coordination problems remained between municipal and county responsibilities — particularly for people with complex, multiple, or rapidly changing needs. This is close to a textbook example of **cross-boundary complexity growing faster than the coordination architecture.**

## Functional scale and boundary fit

Different components of care have genuinely different natural scales — daily care is highly local, home nursing is local, rehabilitation is local-plus-regional, physician access needs a broader specialist system, hospital treatment needs regional/national specialization. So "should elder care be local or regional?" is too coarse a question. The more useful one: **which component belongs at which scale, and how should those components be connected around the individual?** The later government review arrived at essentially this question when it asked whether the municipality/county boundary was actually fit for purpose for people with complex needs. This gives a strong empirical formulation of H14: a governance boundary can be reasonable for organizational specialization while being poorly aligned with the functional trajectory of the person being served.

## Nested centralization

**Assessment: no clear evidence of nested centralization in the narrow sense.** S3 does not show the municipality becoming a centralized internal node suppressing lower-level decision-making. The stronger, more defensible diagnosis is **boundary fragmentation after functional decentralization** — the system moved responsibility downward but left another key function (physicians) at a higher level, creating cross-boundary dependency. This differs importantly from S2, where the risk was internal recentralization *within* the municipality; here it's a persistent seam *between* two co-equal principals with the individual at the intersection.

---

## Observed outcomes

The evidence is distinctly mixed, but not in the simplistic "some studies say good, some bad" sense. The **official retrospective picture is broadly positive**: the 2003 review states experiences were predominantly positive, citing developed quality assurance, strengthened supervision, increased self-determination, and improved cooperation. Socialstyrelsen's 1996 final evaluation similarly found the reform created good conditions for continued development, while documenting remaining weaknesses. **At the same time**, significant coordination problems persisted, especially for complex and rapidly changing needs. **A reform can produce a net-positive structural change and still reveal a persistent architectural defect — there's no contradiction here.**

**Structural transformation** was one of the clearest positive effects: large-scale modernization of special housing, roughly 20,000 new elder dwellings added during the 1990s with state stimulus funding, stronger possibilities for remaining in ordinary housing.

**But the system changed around the reform too**: economic austerity, rapid medical-technological development, fewer hospital beds, shorter hospital stays, and rising medical complexity in municipal care all occurred simultaneously. Geriatric hospital places fell from roughly 8,000 (1992) to roughly 3,200 (2000). Post-1992 problems therefore cannot automatically be attributed to the original reform — the governance environment itself changed.

## The system generated its own repairs

The trajectory is itself a Governance-as-Engineering observation: Ädelreformen (1992) → new governance boundary → new coordination problems → local agreements → Socialstyrelsen guidance (1996) → a formal cooperation inquiry (1999) → Prop. 2002/03:20 → joint committees and mandatory coordinated care planning → SOU 2004:68 asking whether the boundary itself should change further. **The architecture adapted after observing failures at its own interfaces.** These later corrections should be treated as part of the case, not as separate historical events — and they're directly relevant to H8 (cooperation as an alternative to merger) and H15 (dynamic subsidiarity).

---

## Counterevidence / qualifications

- Official evaluations were predominantly positive — this case does not establish that decentralization "failed."
- Health-system, demographic, technological and economic changes during the 1990s make clean causal attribution of post-reform problems to the reform itself very difficult.
- The case doesn't establish that one principal should control all care — it more precisely raises the question of whether shared coordination can sometimes outperform full institutional integration.
- It doesn't establish that local provision is better for every function — highly specialized medical functions plausibly have real scale and competence requirements.

## What this case teaches us

S3 suggests that **functional subsidiarity is not equivalent to assigning an entire policy area to one administrative level.** Healthcare and elder care are not single functions — they contain nested functions with different information structures, specialization requirements, time scales, resource requirements and natural boundaries. A potentially viable architecture is: local care + regional medical expertise + shared coordination + common information + clear responsibility for the individual's pathway. The sharper question isn't "should healthcare be municipal or regional?" but **can a multi-level system preserve the benefits of specialization without fragmenting the person's actual experience?**

A methodologically important lesson follows directly: because Socialstyrelsen and the Government judged the reform predominantly positively *while simultaneously* documenting persistent boundary failures, the Evidence Matrix needs to be able to record "**overall positive outcome + structural failure mode**" simultaneously, rather than forcing every case into a simple good/bad category.
