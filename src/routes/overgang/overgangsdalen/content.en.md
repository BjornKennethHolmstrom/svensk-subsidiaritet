# The Transition Valley

## Where reform temporarily loses capacity — and how to build the bridges

The central distinction is between **destination performance** — how well an architecture works once established — and **transition performance** — how well the system functions while moving from one architecture to another.

A reform can therefore be desirable in its mature form while being dangerous to introduce prematurely. The general pattern looks roughly like this:

```text
Performance
    ↑
 10 │                              ● Mature polycentric architecture
    │                           ／
  8 │         ●─────────●
    │        /           \\
  7 │──────●               \\
    │                        \\
  6 │                         ●
    │
  5 │              TRANSITION VALLEY
    │                    ↓
    └────────────────────────────────────→
             Increasing architectural divergence
```

The central engineering question is therefore:

> **How can bridge institutions be installed early enough that the transition valley becomes shallow, narrow, or disappears entirely?**

---

## Five principal valleys

| Valley | What is lost first | What is supposed to replace it | Required bridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coordination Valley** | Central coordination | Network coordination | Cross-scale/cross-domain coordination |
| **Accountability Valley** | Clear line of responsibility | Distributed accountability | Mandates, provenance, audit, appeals |
| **Speed Valley** | Unified decision path | Parallel decision capacity | Delegation, protocols, emergency escalation |
| **Epistemic Valley** | Simplicity of one knowledge source | Richer knowledge diversity | Evidence institutions + deliberation |
| **Legitimacy Valley** | Familiar institutional legitimacy | More differentiated legitimacy | Citizen participation + transparent role allocation |

These five valleys don't necessarily occur at the same time. That means a transition can be designed so that one capability is strengthened **before** another capability is intentionally weakened.

---

## Valley I — Coordination

The naive transition: strong central coordination → more autonomous institutions → each institution optimizes locally → cross-boundary conflicts → coordination failure → coordination mechanisms eventually get built, too late.

The safe transition is the reverse: central coordination → cross-institution coordination → shared information → common protocols → test network coordination → transfer selected authority → scale network coordination.

The rule:

> **Do not dismantle central coordination until the network can actually coordinate.**

Bridge institutions: cross-sector coordination councils, shared data/model infrastructure, cross-sector scenario exercises, formal escalation mechanisms, interoperability standards.

---

## Valley II — Accountability

Perhaps the most politically dangerous valley. Central government provides a remarkably simple accountability heuristic: *who's responsible? — the Government.* Polycentric governance replaces this with: municipality + region + agency + community + regulator + network. Without design safeguards, responsibility becomes diffuse.

Naive path: central accountability → delegation → overlapping jurisdictions → "not our responsibility" → blame diffusion.

Engineered path: central accountability → explicit delegation rules → decision provenance → independent audit → appeal mechanisms → distributed accountability → polycentric authority.

The critical principle:

> **Every increase in autonomy must be accompanied by an equivalent increase in traceability.**

---

## Valley III — Speed

This valley is subtle because speed means different things. Centralized systems can make a single decision quickly, but can be slow at detecting local problems, processing diverse information, adapting after implementation, and coordinating across specialized domains.

Polycentric systems can eventually become faster because decisions happen closer to the source of information. But initially: **more participants + more coordination = slower decisions.** That produces a classic J-curve — worse before better.

Three mechanisms cross the valley: **delegated authority** (institutions can act without asking the centre for every decision), **predefined escalation thresholds** (instead of asking "who decides?" after a crisis has already started), and **emergency powers with automatic expiry** — triggered, bounded, reviewable and reversible.

---

## Valley IV — Epistemic complexity

This valley initially runs in the opposite direction. A centralized political system is epistemically crude but cognitively simple: one government → one program → one public explanation. Plural governance produces: experts + citizens + communities + political parties + local knowledge + ecological monitoring — epistemically richer, but it initially creates an **integration cost**.

The transition: low epistemic diversity → more information → information overload → need for aggregation mechanisms → higher-order epistemic integration.

The mistake would be to read the temporary complexity as evidence that epistemic diversity itself was a mistake. The actual missing capability is **epistemic integration**: raw evidence → evidence synthesis → scenario modelling → citizen deliberation → policy alternatives → uncertainty representation → decision → evaluation.

The goal isn't "everyone participates in everything." It's: **everyone with relevant knowledge has an appropriate pathway into the decision.**

---

## Valley V — Legitimacy

Perhaps the most psychologically important valley. People understand: elections → Parliament → Government. They don't as readily understand: citizens' assembly → expert council → regional institution → ecological guardian → Parliament → meta-governance.

Even if the latter eventually produces better governance, citizens can experience the transition as: **"nobody seems to know who's actually in charge anymore."**

Every new institution needs a clear answer to: **why does this institution exist? Whom does it represent? What authority does it have? What authority does it not have? Who can override or review it?** That turns institutional differentiation into comprehensible constitutional architecture, rather than confusion.

---

## A diagnostic tool: the valley floor

For every reform, we can ask: **"What is the worst temporary degradation this reform can produce before its intended benefits appear?"**

Example: citizens' assemblies → potential valley floor: decision delay → mitigation: defined remit + deliberative timetable + parliamentary integration.

Local autonomy → potential valley floor: fragmentation → mitigation: common standards + coordination + escalation.

Multiple councils → potential valley floor: institutional conflict → mitigation: jurisdiction matrix + conflict-resolution mechanism.

Bioregional governance → potential valley floor: constitutional ambiguity → mitigation: explicit functional competencies.

---

## A readiness condition

A useful formal heuristic:

```
Readiness = (coordination × accountability × interoperability × knowledge)
            ÷ (complexity introduced by the decentralization)
```

This is **not** meant as a literal empirical law. It's a design heuristic: don't increase institutional complexity faster than the capabilities required to integrate it. A reform becomes progressively safer as readiness rises. (See also **[Adaptive Subsidiarity →](/arkitektur/adaptiv-subsidiaritet/)**, where the same heuristic is introduced from a different angle.)

---

## The most important asymmetry

Removing authority is easy. Building institutional capability is slow. Therefore:

> **Capability-building should normally precede authority redistribution.**

This also gives us a political strategy that isn't merely rhetorical. We can spend years improving evidence systems, citizen deliberation, coordination, accountability and interoperability — without needing to advocate immediate constitutional transformation. When those capabilities become strong enough, more decentralized arrangements become **obviously less risky**. The system itself generates evidence that it can handle more autonomy.
