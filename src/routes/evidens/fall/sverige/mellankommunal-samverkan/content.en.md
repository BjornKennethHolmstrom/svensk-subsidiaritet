# S5 — Swedish Intermunicipal Cooperation

**Country:** Sweden · **Primary period:** 1990s–present, major legal expansion in 2018 · **Domain:** Local government, administrative capacity, specialization, coordination, accountability · **Status:** Finished draft

---

## Case at a glance

Swedish municipalities increasingly use formal cooperation to share functions, specialist competence and resources without merging their political jurisdictions. Evidence from Statskontoret indicates cooperation often strengthens capacity and quality — especially for small municipalities — while simultaneously creating coordination, accountability and democratic-distance costs.

**Why this case matters:** it provides a *third* response to the scale problem. S1 asks *should small municipalities become larger?* S4 asks *can fiscal capacity be pooled without merging decision-making?* S5 asks **can functions be pooled without merging the political units?** Together, these three cases suggest the design space for governance scale isn't one-dimensional.

## Context

Swedish municipalities differ substantially in population size, demographic structure, tax base, geography, specialist labour supply and administrative capacity. Small municipalities can struggle to maintain specialized functions alone. Statskontoret's 2023 analysis explicitly identifies demographic change and urbanization as increasing differences in municipal resources and vulnerability among smaller municipalities — while merger remains politically and democratically costly. Cooperation offers a third architecture.

## What was intended?

Common objectives vary by function but generally include improving administrative capacity, obtaining specialist competence, sharing infrastructure, reducing vulnerability, improving quality, using resources more efficiently, and handling functions whose natural scale exceeds an individual municipality. The 2018 legal reform expanded contractual cooperation specifically to improve municipalities' ability to meet current and future challenges, **including recruiting specialist competence** — cooperation became an explicit instrument of local government policy, not merely a tolerated exception.

## Formal governance mechanisms

Municipalities can cooperate through municipal federations, joint committees, jointly owned companies, or contractual cooperation. The 2018 amendment to the Local Government Act created a general right to contractual cooperation: under Chapter 9, Section 37, a municipality may agree that another municipality performs all or part of one of its tasks, with certain decision authority delegable to an employee in the performing municipality, subject to statutory limits. The law requires the municipal executive board to report annually to the full council on such cooperation.

## Direction of decentralization

S5 is unusual: neither simple decentralization nor centralization. **Political authority** stays distributed among municipalities. **Functional authority** can be pooled between them. **Production capacity** can concentrate in one shared provider. **Accountability** must be reconstructed according to the institutional form chosen. This is a form of **polycentric functional governance.**

## Extent of cooperation

Statskontoret's 2022 survey of all 290 municipalities (185 responses) found more than 9 in 10 reporting contractual cooperation, 8 in 10 belonging to a municipal federation, and almost 7 in 10 to at least one joint committee. Contractual cooperation had grown substantially since 2012. An important data-quality caveat: many municipalities had difficulty obtaining a full overview of their own cooperation agreements — 184 municipalities reported 333 agreements, but the true number was likely higher, which is itself relevant to the accountability picture below.

## Functional distribution

The strongest pattern in the whole case: cooperation is **not evenly distributed across functions**. Among responding municipalities, contractual cooperation was common in social emergency services/family law (72%), environmental and health protection enforcement (56%), upper-secondary education (52%), emergency services/crisis preparedness (48%), procurement (45%), legal competence (35%), adult education (34%), and IT (32%) — but much less common in primary school (15%) and elderly care (7%). This is highly relevant to H7: the functions most often shared tend to be specialized, discrete, legal/regulatory, technological, or emergency/network in character; the functions most often kept local tend to be large, politically salient, continuous, and directly experienced by citizens. This doesn't prove these functions *should* be organized at larger scale — but it shows Swedish municipalities are already acting on an implicit, function-specific scale hypothesis.

## Authority topology by cooperation form

Different institutional forms distribute authority differently. In a **joint committee**, political representation from participating municipalities stays built into the governing arrangement. In a **municipal federation**, a shared body with its own governance structure takes on the task. In **contractual cooperation**, one municipality performs the task for another under agreement, with possible delegation to individual employees. Each form trades off political control against operational simplicity differently.

## Capacity, information and coordination

Capacity gains are the headline finding — see outcomes below. On information: cooperation requires participating organizations to exchange information and coordinate decisions across organizational lines, which is exactly why the next section (trust) matters as much as it does.

## Trust and relational infrastructure

Statskontoret identifies trust, good relations, interaction, formalization and thorough preparation as key success factors — successful cooperation is characterized by high formalization *and* interaction *and* good relationships. This is directly relevant to the R8 (civic/institutional capability) framework: the architecture doesn't operate mechanically. It requires **relational capability** among participating organizations — plausibly a distinct capability variable (boundary-spanning capability / inter-organizational capability) that shouldn't yet be treated as an established construct.

## Accountability topology

This is the main democratic cost. The more municipalities share a function, the harder "who is responsible?" becomes to answer — a citizen may face a chain running municipality → shared institution → another municipality/federation/contracted provider. Statskontoret finds cooperation can reduce municipalities' own opportunities to control the activity, creating real democratic risk: **capacity and specialization up, direct political steering and accountability down** — with the exact trade-off depending on the institutional form chosen.

## Democratic proximity

The effect is ambiguous by design: cooperation can preserve municipality-level political autonomy while reducing direct municipal control over the *shared* function. "The municipality remains responsible" does not necessarily mean "the municipality can meaningfully steer." A useful future measure should distinguish **formal accountability** from **effective controllability** — they are not the same thing.

## Nested centralization

A shared body can become a new functional centre — this is not automatically pathological. Specialist environmental enforcement, for instance, may genuinely benefit from being separated from everyday local political pressure. Whether a given instance represents desirable specialization or undesirable distance can't be judged from topology alone; it requires asking who appoints, who monitors, who can change the mandate, who controls the budget, who can terminate cooperation, and who can appeal decisions. **Assessment: possible / function-dependent**, not inherently negative.

## Scale and specialization

Among the strongest evidence in the whole corpus for H5 and H7: municipalities cooperate disproportionately in functions where specialized competence or a larger production base is valuable (environmental inspection, emergency services, procurement, upper-secondary education, IT, legal competence), and much less in highly citizen-facing functions (primary school, elderly care).

## The 2018 reform as an institutional experiment

The 2018 legal change gives a useful before/after window. Statskontoret later found half of municipalities reported more contractual cooperation after the reform, a quarter attributed the increase to the legal change specifically, smaller municipalities were particularly likely to increase use, and nearly half reported new-task cooperation. This is a useful natural-experiment window — but Statskontoret reports municipalities' *perceptions* of causes, not a randomized causal estimate, so caution is warranted.

---

## What happened to costs?

A finding that directly contradicts a common assumption: cooperation can **improve efficiency but rarely reduces municipal costs**. The mechanism instead often looks like: same/rising resources → more capacity, specialization, quality, lower vulnerability → slower future cost growth. A governance intervention can therefore be economically valuable **without** an immediate budget saving — which should stop "cost per resident" from being used as the sole performance criterion in future measures.

## What happened to quality?

Statskontoret's case studies find examples of cooperation increasing specialization, professionalizing operations, and improving service, quality and resilience — but this rests on **four case studies plus a survey plus prior research**, not a universal causal estimate across all Swedish cooperation. That scope limitation should be stated whenever the finding is cited.

---

## Counterevidence / qualifications

This case does **not** establish that cooperation is universally better than merger, that cooperation is cheaper, that small municipalities are inherently inefficient, or that shared institutions are less democratic. It **does** show real democratic and administrative costs can arise, with magnitude depending on institutional form and function — and it does not establish that the 2018 legal change *caused* all subsequent growth in cooperation, only that municipalities perceive a connection.

## What this case teaches us

S5 may be the clearest Swedish evidence that **subsidiarity need not mean organizational self-sufficiency.** A municipality can retain political identity and local decision-making while participating in larger functional systems for the specific functions where scale or specialization genuinely matter. The critical design problem becomes: **how do we pool functional capacity without unintentionally pooling away accountability, local information, and democratic control?** That's a substantially more precise formulation of polycentric subsidiarity than simply asking whether municipalities should be large or small.

S1, S4 and S5 together suggest a comparative framework the project should keep using: the design space for solving a "scale problem" isn't one-dimensional. Merger pools *all* functions and reduces local political plurality; equalization pools fiscal capacity while retaining local politics; cooperation pools *selected* functions while retaining local politics. Comparing **architectures**, not just "centralization versus decentralization," is the more useful frame.
