# Coordination, Complexity & Accountability

## When does multilevel governance become so complex that coordination, accountability or decision speed deteriorate?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported
**Confidence:** Medium
**Key finding:** Every increase in the number of actors sharing a governance function in this corpus's cases came with a measurable increase in coordination or accountability cost — the question is not whether this trade-off exists, but whether the resulting capability gain is worth it, and whether the cost can be reduced by design.
**Cross-cutting note:** This is one of the corpus's most important interfaces with Governance as Engineering — several GAE formal concepts (coordination tax, boundary instability) are used here as an interpretive lens.

---

## The question

Distributing authority, information or resources across multiple actors can improve fit to local conditions and specialization — but it also multiplies the number of relationships that must be managed. This topic asks:

> At what point does the coordination burden of a multi-actor governance arrangement start to outweigh its benefits, and what architectural features determine that point?

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Coordination burden** — the ongoing cost of maintaining shared understanding, joint decisions, and consistent action across multiple actors.
- **Accountability distance** — how difficult it becomes to trace a specific outcome back to a specific responsible actor as more actors are involved.
- **Boundary permeability** — how easily information and responsibility cross an institutional boundary at the moments that matter (e.g., handoffs, transitions, crises).
- **Adaptive correction** — whether the system can detect coordination failures and build bridging mechanisms in response.

---

## What the evidence shows

**[Ädelreformen (S3)](/evidens/fall/sverige/adelreformen/)** is the clearest coordination case: splitting elder care and healthcare responsibility between municipalities and county councils created persistent problems specifically for people with complex or rapidly changing needs — exactly where cross-boundary coordination mattered most. Critically, the case also shows the system *responding* to this: 1996 information-transfer regulations, a 1999 cooperation inquiry, and Prop. 2002/03:20's joint committees and mandatory coordinated care planning. Coordination cost didn't just appear once and stay fixed — it triggered institutional adaptation.

**[Intermunicipal cooperation (S5)](/evidens/fall/sverige/mellankommunal-samverkan/)** shows the accountability side of the trade-off directly: the more municipalities share a function, the harder "who is responsible?" becomes to answer, and Statskontoret found many municipalities struggled to even maintain a full overview of their own cooperation agreements. Yet the same case shows this cost is manageable — cooperation that invests in trust, formalization and interaction tends to work; cooperation that doesn't, tends not to.

**[Municipal mergers (S1)](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/)** shows a different resolution: reducing the *number* of independent actors (fewer, larger municipalities) reduced one kind of coordination burden (between small municipalities) while creating another (internal coordination and representation within the larger unit) — coordination cost doesn't disappear when actors are merged, it relocates.

**[School decentralization (S2)](/evidens/fall/sverige/skoldecentralisering/)** shows a specific coordination failure mode: when the state removed detailed regulation but retained national goals and evaluation, the system became more dependent on monitoring, evaluation and communication mechanisms — and when those mechanisms were weak (as the 2014 Schools Commission documented), governance gaps appeared without formal responsibility ever being unclear.

---

## Where findings agree

- Every mechanism examined here (splitting responsibility, sharing functions, reducing the number of units, replacing detailed regulation with goal-steering) trades one kind of coordination cost for another rather than eliminating coordination cost outright.
- Systems in this corpus have repeatedly detected coordination failures and responded with bridging mechanisms (joint committees, information regulations, evaluation reforms) — coordination architecture is not fixed at the moment of reform.

## Where findings diverge

- We don't yet have a case with a clean measure of *how large* the coordination cost is relative to the capability gain it buys — the current evidence is directional (costs exist, responses occur) rather than quantitative.

---

## Counterevidence

- Ädelreformen's predominantly positive overall evaluation, despite persistent coordination problems, shows coordination cost doesn't necessarily dominate the outcome — a reform can be net-positive while still having a real, documented coordination weakness.
- Cooperation's ability to work well when relational infrastructure is present (S5) suggests coordination cost is not a fixed penalty of multi-actor governance, but partly a design and investment choice.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The corpus supports treating coordination as **a design variable, not merely a cost** (synthesis claim [C5](/evidens/syntes/vad-vi-vet/)): the size of the coordination burden created by a given governance arrangement depends heavily on whether bridging institutions (information protocols, joint committees, relational infrastructure, evaluation mechanisms) are built deliberately, rather than being a fixed consequence of how many actors are involved.
