# Meeting 3: Subsidiarity – a principle to test

**Goal:** Participants understand what subsidiarity means, see examples of both local and common solutions, and test the principle against their own experiences, including the arguments against it.

**Materials you need:**
- Refreshments
- Printouts of the example cards
- Flipchart

---

## 0–10 min: Welcome and recap

> "Welcome to meeting three! Last time we examined systems with the Proximity Compass. What do you remember? What did the groups conclude?"

Let two or three people answer. Summarise briefly.

---

## 10–30 min: What does subsidiarity mean?

> "Subsidiarity is a complicated word for a fairly simple idea: decisions should be made as close as possible to those affected, but at a higher level when needed for the task to be solved.
>
> The idea has a long history. It was formulated, among other places, in the writings of Catholic social teaching in the 1930s and exists today in the EU treaties, where it governs what the EU should do and what should be left to the member states."

**Write on the flipchart:**

```
SUBSIDIARITY
As close as possible – as high as necessary
```

> "The principle thus points in two directions. It does not say that everything should be local. It says that one should ask: at what level can this be done best?"

### Reasons to make decisions close

- **Local knowledge:** those who live with a problem often know things that are difficult to convey upwards.
- **Different needs:** when people in different places want different things, different solutions can satisfy more people.
- **Participation:** it can be easier to influence and understand decisions made close by.

### Reasons to make decisions higher up

- **Equal rights:** everyone should have the same fundamental rights, regardless of where they live.
- **Impact on others:** when a decision affects others than those making it, for example emissions or the spread of infection, coordination is often needed.
- **Scale and competence:** some things become better or cheaper at a larger scale, and small units may lack specialist knowledge.
- **Risk-sharing:** when costs are shared by many, the individual place copes better in a crisis.

> "Both lists are important. The question is which reasons weigh most heavily for the particular system we are looking at."

## 30–55 min: Example cards

> "Now we'll look at some examples. Each group gets a card. Discuss the questions on the card for 15 minutes and then briefly tell the others."

**Hand out the example cards.** Make sure at least one group gets the card about a common, national solution.

**Reporting back:** 2–3 minutes per group.

---

## 55–75 min: Objections

> "Now we'll test the principle. Here are some common objections. What do you think?"

Read one objection at a time and let the group discuss before you read what others usually answer.

### "Local freedom creates inequality."

> That may be true. If each municipality decides for itself, there can be large differences. Therefore local freedom is often combined with national minimum levels and equalisation between municipalities. The question is where the line should be drawn.

### "It sounds good, but I don't have time."

> Not everything needs to be done by everyone. Many local initiatives are carried by a few. And subsidiarity is not only about voluntary work, but also about where public decisions are made.

### "We centralised for good reasons."

> Yes. Many reforms that gathered decisions or merged units were made to create equivalence, capacity and security. The question is whether the reasons still weigh as heavily for each task, or whether some things would work better closer.

### "This is a way of dismantling the welfare state."

> That is an important objection. Subsidiarity can be used as an argument for the state to withdraw. But the principle also says that a higher level should take responsibility when the lower level cannot manage the task. How to weigh that is a political question.

**Ask the group:**

> "Is there anything in your everyday life where you think decisions should be moved closer? Is there anything where you think it should be more common?"

## 85–90 min: Check-out

---

## If something goes wrong

**The group is very sceptical:** Good. Ask them to formulate their scepticism as clearly as possible and write it down. It is as valuable as the enthusiasm.

**The group is uncritically enthusiastic:** Read the card about the common solution aloud and ask what would happen if it were organised locally.

**Someone wants to discuss the EU instead:** "Subsidiarity is in the EU treaties, but today we're staying with what happens here."
