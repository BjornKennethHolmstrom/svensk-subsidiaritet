# Subsidiarity & Decentralization

## When does decentralized decision-making improve the fit between problems, information, authority and action?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported (not a universal principle)
**Confidence:** Medium
**Key finding:** Local information can improve decisions, but only when decision-makers also have sufficient autonomy and capacity to act on it.
**Key caveat:** Formal decentralization is not a reliable proxy for actual decision proximity — see [How We Know](/evidens/sa-vet-vi/).

---

## The question

Decentralization is often discussed as if it were one thing with one direction. It isn't. This topic asks specifically:

> Under what conditions does moving decision-making authority toward local actors improve outcomes — and under what conditions does it fail to, or make things worse?

That's a narrower and more useful question than "is decentralization good?"

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Local information** — local actors may have information that's costly or impossible for a central authority to obtain.
- **Preference heterogeneity** — different localities may legitimately want different things.
- **Decision proximity** — the actual distance between a decision and the people affected by it, which is not the same as jurisdictional level.
- **Actual autonomy** — whether a local actor can really act on local information, independent of formal responsibility.
- **Initiative** — who can actually start or modify an action.
- **Capacity** — whether the receiving level has the administrative and financial ability to use its authority well.
- **Nested centralization** — the possibility that formal decentralization concentrates decision-making at a *higher* level than intended (e.g., within a merged municipality rather than at the state level).

---

## What the research finds

The literature does not support a simple claim that decentralization improves outcomes. It supports a more conditional pattern: decentralization tends to help when local information genuinely exists and matters, when the receiving level has the capacity to use its new authority, and when the function in question doesn't generate large spillovers requiring coordination. It tends to disappoint, or to look like decentralization without actually being it, when any of those conditions is missing.

A comparative synthesis of decentralization in health and education across low- and middle-income countries finds support for local information mattering, but the effects are conditional rather than universal (SRC-X-002). Separate research on information, governance and service delivery supports distinguishing between information being *available* locally and local actors actually having the *ability or incentive* to act on it (SRC-X-003) — availability alone doesn't guarantee use.

An early and often-cited study of decentralized public investment in Bolivia found that Bolivia's decentralization reform shifted public investment toward areas of greatest need as measured by literacy, and shifted investment toward education, water and sanitation in a way consistent with local government responding to local social needs (Faguet 2004; SRC-X-001). That's a case where the mechanism actually seems to have worked as advertised.

At the same time, our own Swedish case work complicates a purely positive story. The municipal merger reforms (**[Case S1](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/)**) show that a reform explicitly designed to *preserve* local self-government by making the local level more capable simultaneously produced a large *horizontal* concentration of local political authority — the number of municipalities fell from 2,498 to 1,037 (1952) and eventually to 277 (1974/77). Whether that horizontal centralization improved or worsened the fit between decisions and local information remains only partly resolved: a geocoded difference-in-differences study found no overall effect on municipal expenditure after eight years, but did find highly unequal geographic representation within merged municipalities, with smaller, less-represented former municipalities losing local public-sector jobs relative to better-represented areas (Harjunen, Saarimaa & Tukiainen).

---

## Where findings agree

- Local information can matter, but only conditionally — it must be genuinely local, and someone must be positioned to act on it.
- "Decentralization" as a legal or administrative label is not the same as an actual shift in decision-making proximity. Formal and material change need to be assessed separately.
- Capacity at the receiving level is close to a necessary condition, not a nice-to-have.

## Where findings diverge

- Studies differ substantially in what they treat as "decentralization" — fiscal, administrative, political and territorial decentralization are frequently conflated in the broader literature, even though they behave differently.
- Some studies (like Faguet 2004) find decentralization tracking local need well; our Swedish case evidence is much more ambiguous about whether local information actually shaped the fine-grained pattern of outcomes after municipal consolidation.
- Preference-satisfaction gains (people getting what their locality wants) are not the same as welfare gains, and the literature doesn't always keep these apart.

---

## Important conditions

**Tends to work when:**
- local information is genuinely inaccessible or costly to a central authority
- the receiving level has adequate administrative and fiscal capacity
- spillovers to neighboring jurisdictions are limited
- accountability mechanisms exist at the local level

**Tends to fail, or produce only formal change, when:**
- capacity is insufficient at the receiving level
- practical authority, information or initiative remain concentrated even after formal transfer (see H4, "nested centralization")
- the function has large positive or negative spillovers requiring coordination
- the reform is horizontal (fewer, larger local units) rather than vertical (state → locality), which can concentrate rather than distribute authority

---

## Counterevidence

- Comparative decentralization research consistently identifies capacity constraints as a limiting condition, not a footnote — decentralization without capacity is one of the most commonly cited failure modes internationally.
- Our Case S1 evidence directly complicates any simple "decentralization = closer to the people" story: a reform aimed at preserving municipal self-rule produced a dramatic reduction in the number of independent local decision centres.
- Preference heterogeneity (H2) is theoretically well-motivated but not yet directly tested in our current Swedish case set — it should not be treated as established.

---

## Measurement issues

Different studies operationalize "decentralization" differently — as a change in legal responsibility, as a change in spending authority, as a change in the number of jurisdictions, or as a change in who actually initiates decisions. These are not interchangeable, and results from one operationalization don't automatically transfer to another. This is exactly why case-level reconstruction (formal vs. material change, and separate authority/information/resource/initiative topologies) matters more here than aggregate cross-country indices.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The current evidence is more consistent with a **conditional governance-fit proposition** than with a universal decentralization principle: decentralization improves outcomes when local information is genuinely relevant, capacity is sufficient, and spillovers are limited — and can otherwise produce nested centralization, coordination costs, or purely formal change with no material shift in decision proximity. This matches synthesis claim **[C1 — Governance fit](/evidens/syntes/vad-vi-vet/)** in the Evidence Matrix.

We're not yet able to say how large these effects typically are, or how to weigh them against scale and coordination considerations (see the planned R2 and R7 topics) — that requires more comparative case work than currently exists in our corpus.

## Open questions

- How much of Sweden's municipal-merger effect on democratic proximity was compensated for by the later "local organs" reform (1979), which allowed municipalities to devolve some authority to sub-municipal bodies?
- Does the Bolivia-style "decentralization tracks local need" pattern (Faguet 2004) replicate in Nordic or other high-capacity administrative contexts, or is it specific to contexts with larger initial information gaps?
- What would a direct measure of "decision proximity" (as opposed to jurisdiction size) actually look like for Swedish municipalities? See the planned **Decision Proximity** measure under [Data & Models](/evidens/datamodeller/).

## Relevant hypotheses

**H1** — Local information can improve action–condition matching when information is strongly localised and decision-makers have sufficient autonomy/capacity. *(Conditionally supported)*

**H2** — Greater legitimate variation in preferences increases the potential value of local differentiation. *(Unresolved — not yet directly tested in our case set)*

**H3** — Decentralisation is less likely to improve outcomes when receiving capacity is insufficient. *(Supported)*

**H4** — Formal decentralisation does not necessarily create substantive subsidiarity when practical authority, information or initiative remain concentrated. *(Conditionally supported)*

## Relevant GAE concepts

**Requisite variety** — the idea that a governance system needs at least as much internal variety as the problem environment it's trying to manage, which bears directly on when local variation in decision-making is valuable rather than wasteful. See [Paper IV: Requisite Variety and the Commons](https://www.bjornkennethholmstrom.org/working-papers/requisite-variety-and-the-commons).

**Signal fidelity / representational depth** — relevant to how much local information survives a representation chain (state → county board → municipality, in the S1 case). See [Paper III: The Observability-Democracy Connection](https://www.bjornkennethholmstrom.org/working-papers/observability-democracy-connection).
