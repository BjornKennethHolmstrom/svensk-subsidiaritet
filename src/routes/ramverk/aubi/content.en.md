# Adaptive Universal Basic Income (AUBI)

## A research hypothesis about security, heterogeneous needs, and agency

### Why a Subsidiarity Site Is Exploring Basic Income

This page is not primarily about money. It is about **capacity**.

Subsidiarity is not simply a matter of formally moving decisions closer to the people affected by them. Distributed governance also requires people and local institutions to have the time, security, knowledge, and practical ability to participate.

Economic insecurity can constrain that capacity. Experimental research on scarcity suggests that pressing financial concerns can consume substantial cognitive bandwidth. In one influential study, the difference on some cognitive tests was comparable to roughly 13 IQ points. This should not be interpreted as poverty permanently lowering intelligence, but as scarcity potentially reducing the cognitive capacity available for other problems at a given moment.

That raises a governance question:

> **Could a stable economic foundation make it easier for people to exercise the freedom and responsibility that distributed governance requires?**

Adaptive Universal Basic Income, or **AUBI**, is one possible architecture through which to investigate that question.

It is not the only possible solution, and this page does not assume that AUBI works. We treat AUBI as an **institutional hypothesis** that must be compared with other ways of providing security and responding to different needs.

---

### What Is AUBI?

AUBI is best understood as a **family of possible architectures**, rather than a finished benefit system with a predetermined payment level.

Its simplest form consists of two layers:

**1. A universal floor.**
Everyone is covered by an unconditional economic base. Its purpose is to provide a common level of security and reduce the risk of people falling between administrative categories.

**2. An adaptive layer.**
People have different needs. Disability, children, caregiving responsibilities, housing costs, illness, geography, and life stage can make the same transfer provide very different levels of effective security. An adaptive layer attempts to respond to these differences.

A more developed AUBI architecture may also include:

**3. A collective layer.**
Some resources could support shared goods and voluntary activities that reduce needs or increase collective capacity: care, learning, local preparedness, ecological restoration, culture, community building, or other forms of contribution that markets and public institutions do not always capture well.

This third layer is more exploratory than the first two.

---

### The Central Research Question: People Need Different Things

AUBI connects directly to our hypothesis **H2b — need heterogeneity**:

> **When relevant needs vary substantially between people, groups, places, or circumstances, uniform provision may produce poorer fit than a common framework with appropriately adaptive components.**

This is not the same as preference heterogeneity.

Preference heterogeneity concerns people **wanting different things**.

Need heterogeneity concerns people requiring **different resources or conditions to achieve comparable outcomes**.

For example:

* the same income does not provide the same effective capability to a person with substantial disability-related costs and a person without them;
* the same transfer has different effects where housing and transport costs differ greatly;
* a parent with young children may face different resource requirements from a single adult;
* one household may primarily need cash, while another may primarily need access to healthcare, care services, housing, or transport.

Need heterogeneity therefore does not automatically imply that more individualized benefits are better.

The relevant question is:

> **When is the benefit of adaptation greater than the cost of measuring, administering, and governing it?**

---

### Universalism or Targeting?

This is not a simple conflict between two models.

A universal floor has several possible strengths:

* simplicity;
* high coverage;
* low exclusion risk;
* reduced means-testing;
* predictability;
* less dependence on administrative categories.

Needs-responsive provision can meanwhile:

* concentrate resources where needs are greater;
* reduce underprovision among people facing high costs;
* respond more effectively to life stages and temporary crises;
* recognize that the same nominal transfer can have very different real effects.

But adaptation introduces new problems:

* needs must be observed;
* observations can be wrong;
* criteria can miss people or be manipulated;
* administration consumes resources;
* people may need to demonstrate vulnerability;
* the system can become difficult to understand;
* rapid adaptation can reduce predictability.

AUBI therefore attempts to combine two principles:

> **a universal floor that nobody must qualify for, and adaptation where variation in need is important enough to justify it.**

Whether this architecture performs better than alternatives is an empirical question.

---

### What Does Existing Research Tell Us?

There is substantial research on components of AUBI, but much less on the combined architecture.

#### Unconditional cash transfers and basic income

Experiments and programmes across several countries show that unconditional cash transfers can improve some material and psychological outcomes.

Effects nevertheless vary across:

* populations;
* programme designs;
* payment levels;
* time horizons;
* labour markets;
* local economic conditions.

The literature therefore does not justify simple claims that basic income always increases employment, health, entrepreneurship, or participation.

#### Labour supply

Many basic-income and cash-transfer studies find relatively small average labour-supply effects, but results are context-dependent.

People may respond to greater economic security in different ways: working less, changing jobs, studying, starting an enterprise, caring for others, or using their time for other activities.

#### Health and well-being

There are promising findings for outcomes such as psychological well-being and perceived economic security, but effects are not universal and should not be generalized beyond what individual study designs support.

#### Needs-based supplements

Here there is much more adjacent experience than the term AUBI may suggest.

Many welfare systems already combine broad or general provision with supplements related to:

* children;
* disability;
* caregiving;
* housing;
* specific health and support needs.

There is also research on personal budgets, direct payments, and adaptive social protection.

Several AUBI components therefore have empirical predecessors.

#### Full AUBI

As far as we have been able to identify, **the full AUBI architecture described here has not been tested as an integrated system**.

That distinction matters.

Evidence for UBI is not automatically evidence for AUBI.

Evidence for targeted benefits is not automatically evidence for a universal architecture with adaptive layers.

And evidence for ecological or care-related incentives is not evidence for the whole system.

### What Our Simulations Show

As part of the Svensk Subsidiaritet Evidence programme, we have conducted a series of **25 simulations** examining need heterogeneity and adaptive provision.

The simulations compare combinations of:

* universal provision;
* targeted provision;
* adaptive provision;
* need heterogeneity;
* measurement error;
* administrative cost;
* adaptation speed;
* feedback;
* a collective fund;
* social capital;
* real productive capacity.

The results provide **conditional mechanistic support** for H2b.

Within the model, we find that:

**High need heterogeneity increases the value of adaptation.**
When needs differ substantially, an adaptive layer can provide better fit than completely uniform provision.

**Low need heterogeneity increases the value of simple universalism.**
When needs are relatively similar, the gains from adaptation become smaller.

**Measurement error can destroy the adaptive advantage.**
If relevant needs cannot be observed with sufficient accuracy, misclassification can consume much of the benefit of differentiation.

**Administrative costs matter.**
A theoretically superior allocation is not superior if it requires disproportionate resources to administer.

**The objective function matters.**
A system minimizing average underprovision may produce a different optimum from a system explicitly designed to prevent severe deprivation.

**Adaptation can be too fast.**
When observations contain noise, highly responsive systems can become more volatile.

**Collective provision can sometimes substitute for individualized targeting.**
If common interventions genuinely reduce need or build social capacity, they can complement individual transfers.

---

### What the Simulations Do Not Show

The simulations do **not** establish that:

* AUBI works in the real world;
* AUBI is superior to UBI;
* AUBI is superior to Sweden's current welfare architecture;
* targeted systems are generally inferior to universal ones;
* any particular allocation between individual income and collective provision is optimal in reality;
* care credits would work;
* social capital would actually increase;
* any particular AUBI payment level is appropriate;
* the parameter values used in the models correspond to real Swedish conditions.

The simulations are mechanistic tests.

Their most important purpose is to generate **empirically testable predictions**.

---

### What Should We Observe If H2b Is Correct?

Our models generate several predictions that can be compared with real welfare systems.

**1. Greater need heterogeneity should increase the value of adaptive provision.**

**2. Poorer need measurement should reduce the value of adaptation.**

**3. Higher administrative costs should reduce the value of differentiated systems.**

**4. Systems prioritizing severe deprivation should allocate differently from systems optimizing average outcomes.**

**5. Faster adaptive rules should be more responsive but also more volatile under uncertain observations.**

**6. Transfers that alter future need should produce different long-term outcomes from those predicted by static evaluations.**

**7. Collective investments can compete with individual transfers when they effectively reduce future needs.**

**8. The real effect of additional purchasing power should depend on actual supply capacity in housing, healthcare, energy, care, and other constrained sectors.**

These propositions now need empirical investigation.

### AUBI as System Architecture

The systemic argument for AUBI is broader than income distribution.

A person with greater material security may have more practical ability to:

* leave a harmful work situation;
* study;
* care for others;
* recover;
* participate in civil society or local democracy;
* start an enterprise;
* experiment with new forms of contribution;
* refuse dependence on an employer or institution.

This could increase **practical autonomy** within society.

From a Governance as Engineering perspective, this can be described as a possible increase in society's available **variety**: more people become able to pursue a wider range of responses and experiments.

But this remains primarily a mechanistic hypothesis.

Economic security does not automatically produce:

* more participation;
* better decisions;
* more innovation;
* greater social trust.

These outcomes must therefore be studied separately.

---

### Care and Contribution Beyond Paid Employment

A more ambitious AUBI idea is to make additional forms of social contribution visible.

These might include:

* family care;
* mentoring;
* local civic work;
* ecological restoration;
* open-source contribution;
* cultural preservation;
* local preparedness;
* knowledge sharing.

Much of this value is currently produced outside conventional paid employment.

That does not automatically mean that all of it should be **measured and paid**.

The moment a reward is attached to a measure, difficult questions arise:

* What counts as care?
* Who decides?
* How do we prevent favoritism?
* How is privacy protected?
* What happens to intrinsic motivation?
* How do we avoid Goodhart's law — optimizing the metric instead of the underlying value?

We therefore treat a potential care- or contribution-reward layer as **exploratory**, not as a necessary component of AUBI.

---

### The Most Important Risks

AUBI could create new problems even if its underlying intuition proves valuable.

#### Measurement error and exclusion

An adaptive system may miss people whose needs are difficult to observe.

#### Administrative complexity

An excessively detailed adaptive system can recreate the bureaucracy that universalism is intended to reduce.

#### Privacy and surveillance

Needs-responsive provision creates incentives to collect information about people's lives.

#### Goodhart effects

When benefits depend on a metric, individuals and institutions may begin optimizing the metric.

#### Instability

If benefit levels adjust too quickly, households may find planning difficult and the system may react to noise.

#### Capacity constraints

More money does not automatically create more:

* housing;
* healthcare capacity;
* teachers;
* carers;
* energy;
* transport.

Transfers can therefore partly be absorbed by:

* higher prices;
* rents;
* queues;
* bottlenecks.

#### Substitution for public services

AUBI should not become an argument for replacing functioning:

* healthcare;
* education;
* care services;
* housing policy;
* collective infrastructure

with cash.

Some problems are collective and cannot be solved through individual purchasing power.

#### Local capture and favoritism

Local validation can capture local knowledge, but it can also reinforce nepotism, discrimination, and established power relations.

#### Commodification of care

Measuring and rewarding relational care may alter the activity itself.

#### Political instability

A system can be technically viable while remaining politically fragile if governments repeatedly change its funding or rules.

---

### Financing and Real Resources

The question “how much would AUBI cost?” matters, but it is not sufficient.

Economic systems are ultimately constrained by what society can actually produce and organize:

* housing;
* food;
* energy;
* healthcare;
* care;
* transport;
* labour;
* ecological capacity.

A larger transfer does not automatically create more of these resources.

AUBI must therefore be analysed together with:

* taxation;
* monetary policy;
* housing;
* labour markets;
* productive capacity;
* public services;
* demographics;
* ecological constraints.

Possible financing architectures could include restructuring existing transfers, changing tax bases, and other reforms.

This page does not propose a specific Swedish AUBI payment level or a finished financing package.

That requires a separate macroeconomic and public-finance analysis.

### AUBI in the Swedish Context

Sweden has several characteristics that make the question particularly interesting.

**A comprehensive welfare state.**
AUBI would not be introduced into an institutional vacuum. It would interact with sickness insurance, social assistance, housing support, pensions, child-related benefits, and public services.

**High administrative and digital capacity.**
Sweden has strong technical capacity for payments, but this does not automatically make sophisticated needs measurement desirable or legitimate.

**A strong tradition of collective risk pooling.**
The municipal equalization system, for example, illustrates how financing can be pooled at a higher level while operational and political authority remains distributed.

**A strong but changing associational tradition.**
Civil society and local associations could play important roles in a more distributed welfare architecture, but they should not become an excuse for the state to withdraw from basic responsibilities.

**Demographic change.**
An ageing population, changing family structures, and growing care needs make the organization of formal and informal care increasingly important.

These features make Sweden an interesting environment in which to investigate AUBI components — but not automatically an appropriate place for immediate full-scale implementation.

### What Would a Responsible Path Forward Look Like?

Not national implementation.

First, better evidence.

One possible research pathway is:

**Step 1 — Study existing systems.**
Compare universal and needs-responsive provision in real welfare systems.

**Step 2 — Test the model predictions.**
Examine whether need heterogeneity, measurement error, administrative cost, and real capacity affect outcomes in the ways predicted by the simulations.

**Step 3 — Identify which AUBI components already work.**
For example universal benefits, disability supplements, personal budgets, or adaptive social protection.

**Step 4 — Design bounded pilots.**
Where possible, test one mechanism at a time.

**Step 5 — Evaluate independently.**
Measure positive effects, negative effects, and unintended consequences.

**Step 6 — Scale only what survives testing.**

A pilot showing that an AUBI component does not work would also be a valuable result.

---

### Our Current Assessment

AUBI is currently **not an evidence-based finished policy**.

It is a research architecture whose components have very different levels of evidential support.

| Component                        | Current evidence status       |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Unconditional economic floor     | Substantial adjacent evidence |
| Needs-responsive supplements     | Substantial adjacent evidence |
| Universal floor + adaptive layer | Limited direct evidence       |
| Dynamic adaptation over time     | Limited                       |
| Collective fund                  | Exploratory / model-based     |
| Care credits                     | Exploratory                   |
| Ecological incentives            | Adjacent evidence exists      |
| Full AUBI architecture           | Not directly tested           |

Our simulations provide **model support** for the proposition that need heterogeneity can make adaptive components valuable under some conditions.

They do not yet provide empirical support for AUBI as a whole.

---

### Why Continue Investigating the Idea?

Because it asks a larger question:

> **How should economic security be designed so that people do not merely survive, but have real capacity to choose, contribute, recover, and participate — without the state having to define one correct way to live?**

That is an economic question.

But it is also a question of governance, freedom, and institutional design.

AUBI is one possible answer.

We do not yet know whether it is a good one.

That is why we are investigating it.
