# Evidence Standards

## Status vocabulary and editorial rules

This page defines the categories and rules used consistently across the Evidence section. See **[How We Know →](/evidens/sa-vet-vi/)** for the broader epistemological background.

---

## Status vocabulary for hypotheses and synthesis claims

- **Supported** — current evidence broadly supports the hypothesis within its stated scope.
- **Conditionally supported** — evidence supports it under identifiable conditions.
- **Mixed** — credible evidence points in different directions.
- **Unresolved** — insufficient evidence to assess.
- **Weak** — some evidence exists but support is limited or poorly matched.
- **Contradicted** — current evidence conflicts with the hypothesis.

We avoid binary "proven/disproven" language unless genuinely warranted. A hypothesis should not move from "unresolved" to "supported" merely because one new supportive study appears, if meaningful counterevidence remains unexplored.

---

## Five core principles

**1. Mechanisms before labels.** Don't assume something called "decentralization" is substantively decentralized. For every important reform or case, we distinguish formal decentralization, actual decision-making autonomy, information access, resource control, initiative/discretion, implementation capacity, accountability and coordination.

**2. Published research is evidence, not reality itself.** A study can be methodologically strong while measuring a construct that only partially corresponds to the question being asked. We distinguish what the source says, what it actually measures, what mechanism it tests, alternative interpretations, and how closely it maps onto our hypothesis.

**3. Evidence can contradict us.** We actively seek supporting evidence, counterevidence, null results, failed predictions, alternative explanations and unresolved questions. The corpus should not be a collection of arguments for subsidiarity.

**4. Models are not empirical evidence.** Formal and computational results may be rigorous within an explicit model while saying little directly about real institutions. We keep empirical finding, model result, simulation result and interpretive institutional implication separate.

**5. Current knowledge remains revisable.** Every synthesis claim is treated as updateable.

---

## What we do

- distinguish observation from interpretation
- distinguish formal from material change
- expose uncertainty
- include counterevidence
- preserve failed predictions
- link to primary sources
- state scope conditions
- identify alternative explanations
- revise claims when new evidence arrives

## What we don't do

- call every decentralization reform "subsidiarity"
- infer real-world truth directly from simulations
- treat publication as proof
- hide negative results
- manufacture certainty
- use one study as decisive when the literature is mixed
- conflate preference satisfaction with welfare
- assume local is automatically better
- assume larger scale is automatically better

## Source registry

Every source used in the Evidence corpus has a unique, stable ID (e.g. `SRC-S1-001`) and is recorded with author/institution, year, source type, geography, domain, URL/DOI, which content objects it's used in, and a verification status (`verified`, `needs_recheck`, `planned`). That makes every claim traceable back to a specific, identifiable source — see, for example, the sources listed in the **[municipal mergers case →](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/)**.

---

## The traceability requirement

Every important synthesis statement should be traceable:

```text
Synthesis claim
      ↓
Hypothesis
      ↓
Studies / cases / models
      ↓
Sources
```

A visitor should be able to move backward from a high-level claim to the underlying evidence. That's one of the most important functional requirements of the whole Evidence section.
