# Food system: efficiency, preparedness and the right level

## How much of the food can Sweden produce itself?

It is often said that Sweden is self-sufficient in about half of its food. However, the Swedish Board of Agriculture has chosen not to calculate any such total figure, and speaks instead of supply capacity for different products. It varies greatly. For dairy products, calculated in milk equivalents, supply capacity was 75 percent, and for carrots close to 100 percent, while for meat it is flattening out or declining. A total figure thus hides large differences between products.

There is an even more important vulnerability than the share of imported food: Swedish food production is itself dependent on imported inputs such as fuels, mineral fertiliser, feed and chemicals. Even food produced in Sweden can therefore be affected by disruptions in the outside world.

## Preparedness after decades of dismantling

The national goal of self-sufficiency was dismantled in 1989, and the emergency stockpiles were dismantled during the 1990s. Since then, food preparedness has been rebuilt as part of civil defence. The goal is that food supply should be secured for at least three months in a security policy crisis in which trade with the outside world is limited. The government has stated that the build-up starts from a low level.

How preparedness is best strengthened is debated. Some advocate a goal of higher self-sufficiency. Others, including researchers at AgriFood in Lund, argue that it is considerably cheaper to maintain emergency stockpiles of inputs and food than to strive for self-sufficiency.

## Concentration in retail

Grocery retail in Sweden is dominated by a few actors. ICA has about half the market, and ICA, Axfood and Coop together have the largest share, while Lidl and other actors share the rest. Axfood has gained market share in recent years, among other things through Willys, and bought City Gross in 2024. Earlier versions of this page stated that three chains control 95 percent. That is probably too high, but concentration is high.

Concentration affects which producers can reach consumers, because large chains often require large and even volumes. At the same time, it provides efficient distribution and relatively stable prices under normal conditions.

## The cybernetic reading

Food production faces great variation: weather, soil, diseases, markets and crises. A system optimised for low costs under normal conditions can have little margin when something unexpected happens. But that does not mean that small-scale is always better:

- **Large-scale operation and coordination** provide efficiency, better control of food safety and the ability to move food from surplus to deficit.
- **Diversity and local capacity** provide more independent parts, which can function even if one link in the chain fails.

Resilience requires both. The question is where redundancy does the most good per krona: in local production and processing, in emergency stockpiles, in diversity of suppliers or in households’ own capacity.

## Remaining problems

- **Dependence on inputs**, which makes even domestic production vulnerable.
- **Few new farmers**, partly because of high capital requirements and low profitability.
- **Difficult for small producers to reach the market** and to find processing capacity, such as slaughter and dairy.
- **Low preparedness**, which is now being built up from a low level.

## An important legal limitation

Earlier versions of this page proposed that municipalities should procure 25 percent locally produced food and that “local” should be defined in law, for example as within 50 kilometres. That is not compatible with the rules on public procurement, which are based on EU law and prohibit suppliers being favoured because of where they are located. Municipalities can, however, set requirements for, for example, animal welfare, freshness, season, environment and delivery terms, and split procurements so that smaller producers can submit bids. The proposals below are based on that.

## Subsidiarity-based proposals

The proposals are ideas to test in limited pilots with evaluation. Each proposal has its own page.

## Objections

| Objection | How the proposals need to address it |
|---------|------|
| Small-scale production is more expensive | Yes, often. The proposals aim to complement, not replace, and should be compared with other ways of increasing preparedness, such as stockpiles. |
| Sweden cannot feed itself | Capacity varies greatly between products. Preparedness is about production, stockpiles, inputs and distribution. |
| Public procurement cannot favour local producers | Correct, not directly. But requirements for quality, animal welfare and splitting into smaller parts can make it easier for small producers to participate. |

## Conclusion

Sweden’s food system is efficient under normal conditions but has limited margins in the event of major disruptions, and preparedness is now being rebuilt after having been dismantled. Subsidiarity here does not mean that everything should be produced locally, but that redundancy should be distributed wisely: national emergency stockpiles and requirements, regional processing capacity, local sales channels and households that can manage for a time on their own.

### Three things you can do

**If you are a municipal politician:** Find out how the municipality’s food procurement is designed, and whether it can be split so that smaller producers can submit bids.

**If you are a farmer:** Investigate the possibilities for direct sales via REKO rings or farm shops, and for joint processing with other producers.

**If you are a consumer:** Follow the authorities’ advice on home preparedness and keep at least a week’s worth of food at home.
