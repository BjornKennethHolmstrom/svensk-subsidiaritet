# Governance & Adaptation

## Which governance systems can detect, learn from and correct their own failures?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported (as a pattern) / unresolved (as a causal mechanism)
**Confidence:** Low–Medium
**Key finding:** Several Swedish cases show governance systems detecting a mismatch and building a correction — but the corpus does not yet have direct evidence that these corrections actually improved outcomes, only that they occurred.
**Relationship to R8:** Institutional adaptive capacity plausibly depends partly on the capabilities of the people operating the institutions — this topic and R8 should eventually be read together.

---

## The question

A governance architecture that gets things right at the moment of design will inevitably drift out of fit as circumstances change. This topic asks:

> Which institutional features let a governance system notice that its own allocation of authority, information or resources is no longer working — and actually change it?

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Monitoring and evaluation** — institutional capacity to observe outcomes and compare them against goals.
- **Feedback-driven correction** — using that observation to change the governance arrangement itself, not just individual policies within it.
- **Adaptation bottleneck** — the possibility that the slowest link in the sense→learn→act chain limits how fast the whole system can adapt, regardless of how fast other parts are.
- **Formula/model revision** — the specific case of an institution periodically updating a quantitative model of reality (e.g., a funding formula) as that reality changes.

---

## What the evidence shows

Three of the corpus's five cases show a real adaptive-correction event:

**[Municipal mergers (S1)](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/):** the 1979 "local organs" legislation let municipal councils devolve certain functions to district councils and similar sub-municipal bodies — a direct institutional response to the democratic-proximity deficit the mergers had created. But the council retained ultimate control over the local organs' remit, so this was a partial, bounded correction, not a full reversal.

**[Ädelreformen (S3)](/evidens/fall/sverige/adelreformen/):** the clearest adaptive sequence in the corpus — 1996 information-transfer regulations, a 1999 cooperation inquiry, Prop. 2002/03:20's joint committees and mandatory coordinated care planning, and a 2004 review that asked whether the municipality/county boundary itself should change further. The architecture visibly adapted after observing failures at its own interfaces.

**[Fiscal equalization (S4)](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunalekonomisk-utjamning/):** the clearest quantitative case — the state periodically revises the equalization formula (2005, 2014, 2020 reforms) in response to evaluation findings, most directly the 2018 inquiry that fed into the 2020 reform after the Riksrevisionen documented systematic undercompensation.

---

## Where findings agree

- Swedish governance institutions have repeatedly built correction mechanisms after observing a mismatch between the original design and outcomes — this is not a single anomalous event but a recurring pattern across multiple, unrelated policy domains.
- None of these corrections fully reversed the original reform; they were bounded adjustments layered onto the existing architecture rather than wholesale redesigns.

## Where findings diverge

- The corpus does not yet contain outcome data showing whether these corrections actually improved the situation they targeted — we can document that adaptation *occurred*, not yet that it *worked*.

---

## Counterevidence

- The bounded nature of every correction found so far (council retains control over local organs; joint committees added without changing the underlying two-principal structure; formula revisions rather than architectural redesign) is itself a caution against overstating institutional adaptability — Swedish governance adapts at the margin more readily than it has been observed to adapt structurally.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The pattern here is suggestive of **H15 (a resilient subsidiarity architecture should be able to change authority allocation as problem structure changes)**, but H15 remains formally "Unresolved" in the Evidence Matrix precisely because we lack outcome evidence for whether the corrections improved things. This topic is a strong candidate for the corpus's highest-priority evidence gap: longitudinal, before/after outcome data spanning a correction event (see [What We Don't Know](/evidens/syntes/okanda/)).
