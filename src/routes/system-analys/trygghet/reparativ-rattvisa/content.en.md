# Restorative justice

[← Back to the main article](..)

**Summary:** For less serious crimes, where the offender has admitted the offence and the victim wants to participate, a mediation meeting can lead to an agreement on an apology and compensation. If the agreement is fulfilled, the prosecutor can take it into account in the decision on prosecution. The proposal builds on the mediation in connection with crime that already exists in Swedish law and extends it from mainly young people to more offenders.

*This is a proposal to test in limited pilots with evaluation. The figures below are proposals and rough estimates, not results.*

The page describes two levels. The **pilot proposal** largely fits within current law. The **exploratory variant** further down goes further: there, a fulfilled mediation replaces prosecution even for adults, which requires legislative change.

## Why

In an ordinary legal process, the victim often has a limited role, and the offender rarely faces the consequences of their action directly. Restorative justice starts from the premise that a crime harms people and relationships, and that the harm should as far as possible be repaired. Research reviews, for example Sherman and Strang (2007), have found that mediation meetings between victim and offender often produce high satisfaction among victims and in several studies a certain reduction in recidivism. However, the effects vary between types of crime and programmes.

## What already exists

- **The Act on Mediation in Connection with Crime** (2002:445) regulates how mediation is carried out. Mediation is voluntary for both parties.
- **Municipalities must offer mediation** to young offenders.
- **Waiver of prosecution:** the prosecutor may in certain cases refrain from prosecuting, for example for minor offences (Code of Judicial Procedure, Chapter 20, Section 7). Special rules exist for young people.
- **Sentencing:** the fact that the offender has tried to compensate for or limit the harm can be taken into account when the penalty is determined.

Mediation is nevertheless used to a limited extent, and is today mainly aimed at young people.

## The proposal

**Conditions:**
- the offender has admitted the offence, or the evidence is clear
- the victim wants to participate, entirely voluntarily
- the crime is of a less serious kind, where the penalty would likely be a fine or a conditional sentence
- a trained, impartial mediator leads the process

**The process:**
1. The prosecutor assesses that the case is suitable and refers it to a mediation service.
2. The mediator meets the parties separately and explains what mediation involves. Both must say yes.
3. The parties meet under the mediator’s guidance, in a neutral venue.
4. If they reach an agreement, for example on an apology, compensation or work, it is written down.
5. The mediator follows up that the agreement is fulfilled.
6. The prosecutor takes the result into account in the decision on prosecution. If the agreement is not fulfilled, or if either party withdraws, the case is handled as usual.

**Crimes that may be included (proposal):** petty theft, minor damage, minor assault without serious injury, less serious cases of unlawful threat without weapons, arbitrary conduct.

**Crimes that always take the usual route:** violence with weapons, aggravated assault, sexual offences, crimes in close relationships, crimes against children and repeated serious crime. Crimes in close relationships are excluded because the power imbalance between the parties makes mediation unsuitable.

### What we know

- Mediation in connection with crime is regulated in the Act on Mediation in Connection with Crime and is voluntary for both parties. Municipalities must offer mediation to young offenders.
- Prosecutors can already in certain cases refrain from prosecuting, and the fact that the offender has tried to compensate for the harm can be taken into account when the penalty is determined.
- A systematic review from the Campbell Collaboration (Strang et al. 2013) covered ten randomised studies of mediation meetings, where victims and offenders consented before being randomised to mediation or ordinary processing. Offenders who participated in mediation meetings committed on average fewer new offences, and victims were more satisfied with how their case was handled. The effect was on average moderate.

### What we assume

- That more cases would go to mediation if prosecutors received training and clear routines, and that resources are currently the main limitation.
- That results from international programmes, which have often concerned young people, can be transferred to adult offenders in Sweden.

### What we do not know

- How large the demand is among victims in cases involving adults.
- Whether prosecutors will take fulfilled agreements into account in practice, when there is no explicit rule about it.

### How we would test it

- **Test:** three regions over three years, with comparison against similar cases handled in the usual way.
- **Stop if:** victims in the follow-up report that they felt pressured to participate, in more than isolated cases and without the routines being corrected.
- **Adjust if:** few cases are sent to mediation, or if a large proportion of the agreements are not fulfilled.
- **Continue if:** victims’ experience is at least as good as in the comparison group, a clear majority of the agreements are fulfilled and recidivism is not higher.

## Pilot proposal

**Scope:** Three regions over three years, with a few municipalities in each.

**Organisation:**
- The Swedish Prosecution Authority trains prosecutors in assessing which cases are suitable
- mediation services are expanded from those the municipalities already have for young people, in cooperation with, for example, Victim Support Sweden
- the mediators undergo special training (proposal: 80 hours)
- an independent group follows up quality and handles complaints

**Evaluation:** compare with similar cases handled in the usual way, with regard to victims’ experience of redress, the proportion of agreements fulfilled, recidivism after one and two years, processing time and cost.

## Legal issues to investigate

1. **Link to prosecution:** It should be investigated whether the current rules on waiver of prosecution are sufficient, or whether an explicit provision is needed that a fulfilled mediation agreement should be taken into account.
2. **Mediation for adults:** The Mediation Act already applies regardless of age, but resources and routines exist mainly for young people. A pilot primarily requires funding and organisation, not necessarily new law.
3. **Confidentiality:** What is said during mediation needs to be protected, while the duty to report serious crimes against children remains.

## Objections

**“That is letting criminals go free.”** Mediation requires the offender to take responsibility and compensate for the harm. It does not replace punishment for serious crimes. But it goes against the recent direction in Swedish criminal policy, with stricter penalties and reduced sentence discounts for young people, and that tension should be reported openly.

**“The victim may feel pressured.”** Participation must be entirely voluntary, and the victim must be able to withdraw at any time without consequences for the case.

**“The offender pretends to regret it.”** An agreement requires concrete actions that are followed up. If it is not fulfilled, the case is handled as usual.

**“Prosecutors will not use the possibility.”** Without training and clear routines, that is a real risk.

## Experience to build on

- **Mediation in connection with crime in Sweden**, which municipalities offer to young offenders.
- **New Zealand** has since 1989 used so-called family group conferences for young offenders.
- **The Norwegian Mediation Service (Konfliktrådet)**, where the prosecutor can transfer cases to mediation instead of prosecution. See the exploratory variant below.

## Exploratory variant: mediation instead of prosecution

The pilot proposal lets the prosecutor *take into account* a fulfilled mediation. The exploratory variant goes one step further: for certain less serious crimes, where both parties consent and the agreement is fulfilled, **the mediation replaces the prosecution**, even for adult offenders.

### How it would work

- **Types of crime:** the same less serious crimes as in the pilot proposal, with an identifiable victim.
- **Consent:** both the victim and the suspect must consent. The victim can withdraw at any time.
- **Procedural bar:** if the agreement is fulfilled, prosecution may not be brought for the same act.
- **If the mediation is interrupted:** the case is handled as usual, and what was said during the mediation may not be used as evidence.
- **Limitation:** the possibility may be used once during a certain period, for example five years, so that it does not become a way of repeatedly avoiding trial.
- **Registration:** a fulfilled mediation is registered, but not as a conviction. The proposal is to follow the Norwegian model, where a fulfilled agreement does not appear in an ordinary extract, but where the information is retained so that the limitation to once per period can be applied and so that it can be shown in a criminal record check for work with children.

### There is a model

In Norway, the prosecutor can, when guilt is considered proven, decide that a case should be transferred to mediation in the mediation service instead of being prosecuted. This requires that both the victim and the suspect consent. The mediation services have been used since the 1980s and were enshrined in law in 1991. Today there are twelve mediation services that together cover the whole country, and in 2023 they handled 1,879 mediations in criminal cases. If the suspect enters into and fulfils an agreement, the case is not registered in an ordinary extract from the Norwegian register.

In Denmark, mediation services have been used since 2010 in all police districts, but as a complement to the ordinary process, not as an alternative to prosecution.

The variant is thus not untested in the Nordic countries. It is exploratory here because it requires legislative change in Sweden and goes beyond how mediation is used in Sweden today.

### What would need to change

- The Code of Judicial Procedure’s rules on the duty to prosecute and waiver of prosecution, so that a fulfilled mediation becomes a bar to prosecution.
- The Act on Mediation in Connection with Crime, so that the mediation has this legal effect and so that statements made during mediation cannot be used as evidence.
- The rules on the criminal record.

The EU’s Victims’ Rights Directive (2012/29/EU) already sets requirements for safeguards in restorative justice, including voluntary and informed consent and the right to withdraw it at any time. Those requirements would apply fully.

### What we know

- Norway has a legally regulated arrangement where the prosecutor can transfer cases to mediation in the mediation service instead of prosecution, with consent from both parties.
- The systematic review from the Campbell Collaboration found in ten randomised studies fewer new offences among offenders who participated in mediation meetings, and higher satisfaction among victims. The effect on recidivism was greater for violent crimes than for property crimes. Victims who participated more often received an apology they experienced as sincere, had less desire for revenge and fewer symptoms of post-traumatic stress.
- In Norway, a fulfilled agreement in the mediation service does not appear in an ordinary extract from the register, but it can appear in the most extensive extracts.
- The EU’s Victims’ Rights Directive sets out safeguards for restorative justice.

### What we assume

- That someone who knows that a fulfilled mediation replaces prosecution has stronger reasons to participate and to fulfil it, which would produce more and better-fulfilled agreements.
- That victims who get to meet the offender and influence the compensation in many cases experience greater redress than from a fine.
- That the Norwegian experiences can be transferred to Swedish conditions. We have not found any Norwegian evaluation of recidivism after mediation in the mediation service. The proposal is therefore based on international randomised studies, where mediation was usually a complement to or a diversion from the ordinary process, and on the Norwegian experiences of how the arrangement works in practice.

### What we do not know

- **How the whole behaves.** When mediation replaces prosecution, more is at stake for the suspect. It can make participation more considered, but also more tactical: remorse can become a means rather than an end.
- **New ways of failing.**
  - *Pressure:* the more the suspect has to gain, the greater the risk that the victim is subjected to pressure to consent.
  - *Buying one’s freedom:* if the compensation is often financial, those who have money can more easily avoid prosecution than those who do not.
  - *Expanded control:* cases that would otherwise have been dropped can instead be sent to mediation, so that more people end up in a criminal justice process.
  - *Confidence:* the public may perceive the arrangement as too lenient, which can affect confidence in the justice system.
- How large the demand is, and whether the mediation services would cope with the volume.
- **Which crimes should be included.** The variant is limited here to less serious crimes. But research suggests that the effect on recidivism can be greater for violent crimes. The limitation is thus based on an assessment of what is reasonable and safe to test first, not on evidence about where the effect is greatest.

### How we would test it

- **Test:** a time-limited trial law for three years at a few prosecutor’s offices, with careful selection of types of case. If it is ethically and legally possible, cases where both parties consented are randomised between mediation and ordinary processing, as has been done in international studies of mediation meetings. Otherwise, comparison offices are used.
- **Stop if:** victims in the follow-up report pressure in more than isolated cases and the safeguards cannot prevent it; or if recidivism is clearly higher than in the comparison group; or if it turns out that the possibility in practice is mainly used by those who can pay.
- **Adjust if:** the agreements are often not fulfilled; or if the types of case turn out to be too broad or too narrow; or if the mediation services cannot keep up.
- **Continue if:** victims’ experience is at least as good as in the comparison group, a clear majority of the agreements are fulfilled, recidivism is not higher, and no signs of pressure or economic bias have emerged.

The threshold values are to be determined before the trial starts and not changed afterwards.

### The biggest objection

The variant goes against the recent direction in Swedish criminal policy, with stricter penalties and reduced sentence discounts for young people. It also raises a question of principle: can a crime be handled between those involved, or is a crime always a matter for the whole of society? That question cannot be settled with evidence. A trial can, however, show what happens in practice, and that is the reason the variant is presented here.

## What you can do

**If you are a municipal politician or civil servant:** Examine how often the municipality’s mediation service is used today, and what would be required for it to receive more cases.

**If you work in the justice system:** Find out how mediation is used in your area and what obstacles exist.

**If you are a lawyer, social worker or similar:** Investigate the possibility of training as a mediator.
