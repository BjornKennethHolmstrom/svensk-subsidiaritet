# Decision Proximity

**Status: proposed / not yet validated.** This measure is defined here as a working framework for future data collection — it has not yet been calculated for any Swedish case with real data. Treat it as a specification, not a result.

---

## What does it measure?

The actual distance between a decision and the people affected by it — as distinct from jurisdiction size, administrative level, or formal responsibility, none of which reliably indicate how close a decision really sits to those it affects.

## Why measure it?

This is the single highest-priority evidence gap identified in the corpus (see [What We Don't Know](/evidens/syntes/okanda/)). Nearly every case in this project depends on distinguishing *formal* decentralization from *actual* decision proximity, but that distinction has so far been argued qualitatively, case by case, rather than measured consistently. Without a working measure, claims like "S1 reduced democratic proximity" or "S2 didn't reach effective local autonomy" remain persuasive narratives rather than comparable, falsifiable measurements.

## Conceptual definition

Decision proximity is low when many institutional or representational layers separate an affected person from the actor who actually makes the relevant decision, and high when few layers separate them — but it is not simply "distance in kilometers" or "number of administrative levels." A decision made by an elected local council two administrative layers away can have higher proximity than a decision nominally delegated to a "local" body whose remit, budget and staff are fully controlled by a distant principal (compare the [S1 local-organs correction](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/), where formal proximity increased but the municipal council retained ultimate control).

## Proposed operational definition

A composite built from at least three components, evaluated per decision type rather than per jurisdiction as a whole:

1. **Representation depth** — number of institutional layers between the affected person and the final decision-maker (e.g., citizen → ward → municipal council → committee → administrator).
2. **Discretion at the point of contact** — how much genuine decision-making latitude exists at the layer closest to the affected person, as opposed to that layer merely implementing a decision made elsewhere.
3. **Responsiveness lag** — how long it takes for a locally observed problem to reach a decision-maker with the authority to act on it.

## Units

Not yet standardized. Representation depth is a simple integer count; discretion and responsiveness lag would need their own sub-scales, which is itself an open methodological question.

## Data sources (proposed)

Formal organizational charts and delegation rules (for representation depth); budget and staffing data at the point of contact (for discretion); case-tracking or complaint-resolution timelines (for responsiveness lag). Swedish municipal and county organizational documentation is likely the most accessible starting point.

## Known limitations

- The three components may not combine linearly, and their relative weights are unknown.
- Representation depth is the easiest component to measure and the least informative on its own — see the S1 local-organs example above.
- Discretion is difficult to observe directly and may require case-by-case qualitative coding rather than a simple numeric scale, at least initially.

## Illustrative (not yet measured) example

**[Case S1](/evidens/fall/sverige/kommunsammanslagningar/)** provides the clearest illustrative case for why this measure is needed: formal representation depth clearly changed (225,000 → 42,000 elected officials), but the *effective* proximity change is harder to establish, since the 1979 local-organs reform partially restored proximity for some functions while leaving ultimate council control intact. A working Decision Proximity measure would let this be stated as a number, or at least a coded qualitative scale, rather than only a narrative.
