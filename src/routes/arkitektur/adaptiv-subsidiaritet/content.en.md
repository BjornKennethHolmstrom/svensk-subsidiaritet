# Adaptive Subsidiarity

## The right decision, at the right level — not automatically the lowest one

Subsidiarity is often misread as a normative claim: *"decisions should be made as locally as possible."*

That is not what we mean.

If a municipality handles something locally that it clearly can't handle well, pushing the decision down further is bad governance. If the state centrally manages something where local communities have much better knowledge, that centralization is bad governance.

The actual principle is:

> **Decisions should be made at the level that can handle them best, given knowledge, externalities, coordination needs, capacity, legitimacy and accountability.**

It is a function of several variables rather than a direction — roughly:

```
Where should decision authority reside?
    = f(scale, knowledge, externalities,
        coordination needs, stakes, accountability,
        adaptability)
```

That is considerably more sophisticated than classical subsidiarity. We call it **adaptive subsidiarity**.

---

## Conditional, not ideological

This means the system can say:

> "This function isn't ready to be decentralized yet."

without that being an anti-subsidiarity position. It is **conditional subsidiarity**.

The question is therefore not:

> "Can this be moved downward?"

but:

> **"Does moving it downward improve system performance while preserving coordination and accountability?"**

---

## A function can move in any direction

A function can move:

**downward → upward → sideways → downward again**

depending on what evidence and experience show. That makes subsidiarity **adaptive rather than dogmatic**. If decentralization produces worse outcomes, we don't romanticize it — we move the function back upward.

---

## A readiness condition

A simple design condition summarizes the principle:

```
Readiness = (coordination × accountability × interoperability × knowledge)
            ÷ (complexity introduced by the decentralization)
```

A reform becomes progressively safer as readiness grows. This formula isn't meant as a literal empirical law — it's a design heuristic: **don't increase institutional complexity faster than the capacity to integrate it.**

---

## Why this answers one of the strongest objections

An establishment critic might say:

> "Sure, a beautiful polycentric system sounds nice, but Sweden isn't going to dismantle the state and hand everything to local councils."

The answer is: **that isn't what we're proposing.**

We're proposing to build the capabilities that make more distributed governance safe — and then, where evidence shows a function is handled better closer to those affected, shift authority there. That is a **falsifiable** hypothesis, not a promise. If decentralization produces worse outcomes, the function moves back.

That turns subsidiarity into an empirical design problem rather than an ideological commitment.
