# Swedish Reform Path

## From principle to a concrete order

The previous pages describe *why* the order matters. This page describes what it can look like in practice: a phased reform order, six hard safeguards, and a simple test to run every individual reform against.

---

## Six safeguards

The following should be treated as **hard engineering constraints**, not aspirational principles.

**1. Never decentralize faster than accountability.** If authority moves downward, accountability infrastructure must move first, or simultaneously.

**2. Never remove coordination before replacement coordination exists.** This directly addresses the transition valley. Don't weaken central coordination in the hope that decentralized coordination will emerge on its own — build network coordination, test it, then transfer authority.

**3. Prefer reversible experiments.** Pilot → measure → expand / modify / reverse, where possible. This dramatically lowers transition risk.

**4. Maintain emergency capacity.** Even a deeply polycentric system needs escalation mechanisms capable of temporarily concentrating authority during genuine emergencies. Subsidiarity does not imply the absence of emergency hierarchy.

**5. Maintain rights floors.** Local autonomy must not permit violation of fundamental rights or systematic exclusion. Some guarantees necessarily remain cross-scale.

**6. No automatic decentralization.** A function can move downward → upward → sideways → downward again, depending on what the evidence shows. That makes subsidiarity genuinely adaptive rather than a one-time event.

## A reform scorecard: six questions

For every proposed reform, we can ask six questions:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Performance** | Does it solve a present governance problem? |
| **Capability** | Does it create a capability needed later? |
| **Reversibility** | Can we undo it if it fails? |
| **Coordination** | Does it preserve or improve system integration? |
| **Accountability** | Does responsibility remain traceable? |
| **Path dependence** | Does it make future improvements easier or harder? |

A reform that scores highly across all six is an ideal **bridge reform**. A reform that improves one dimension while seriously degrading coordination or accountability should be treated with suspicion — even if it looks ideologically attractive.

## A politically realistic starting list

A credible Swedish reform agenda can lead with reforms that don't sound "GGF-ish" at all:

**"Better government intelligence."** — An independent systems-evidence function.

**"Stronger parliamentary capacity."** — Committees with their own analytical capacity and the ability to negotiate across party lines separately from government formation.

**"Citizen deliberation for complex, long-term decisions."** — Permanent citizens'-assembly infrastructure with a clearly bounded mandate.

**"Less siloed public administration."** — Cross-sector coordination councils and shared data between agencies.

**"Clearer accountability for delegated decisions."** — Explicit mandates, decision provenance, independent review.

**"Smarter distribution of responsibility between national, regional and local levels."** — Subsidiarity reviews before, not instead of, decisions.

**"Experimental governance with measurable outcomes."** — Pilot projects with clear evaluation criteria and a predetermined plan for scaling or reversal.

Only later does the vocabulary need to naturally evolve toward **polycentric governance → nested governance → meta-governance → adaptive governance architecture.**

The destination doesn't have to be hidden. But **the capability dependencies should determine the order.**

## A concrete first focus

Rather than aiming directly at architectures 11–12 in the **[Governance Spectrum](/arkitektur/spektrum/)**, a particularly promising first reform track is to ask:

> **Can Sweden be governed without a governing majority — better than it is today?**

That's a question that's already live every time a general election fails to produce a clear majority bloc, it requires no constitutional amendment to start exploring, and it leads naturally into architectures 4–6: issue-by-issue parliamentarism, a more coordinating executive, and citizens' assemblies as a complement to ordinary parliamentary debate.

That gives a concrete, bounded laboratory — rather than an abstract debate about Sweden's constitutional order as a whole — where the first bridge institutions (an evidence function, cross-party negotiation capacity, clearer accountability rules) can be tested in practice.

## The summary

> **Preserve → strengthen → connect → test → delegate → integrate → adapt.**

That's the difference between weak gradualism ("change things slowly") and transition engineering ("change things in dependency order"). The route to polycentric governance is not to weaken the centre first. It's to make the whole system capable of functioning with less dependence on any single centre — which is not the same as a weaker centre. It's a centre that is **less dependent on being central**.
