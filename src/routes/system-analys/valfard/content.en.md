# Welfare: control, trust and the right level

## A large system with two missions

In 2024, Sweden spent just under 28 percent of GDP on social protection, a total of just over SEK 1,770 billion. That is a high level even by European comparison, but lower than in, for example, France and Finland. The largest share goes to older people, mainly pensions.

Welfare rests on two different logics. The social insurance schemes, such as sickness benefit and parental benefit, are national rights administered by the Swedish Social Insurance Agency. Social services, elderly care and support for people with disabilities are municipal and are based to a greater extent on individual assessment. That division is itself an example of subsidiarity: insurance against loss of income works best with a large risk pool and equal rules, while everyday support requires knowledge of the individual’s situation.

## The tension between control and trust

Two lines of development pull in different directions:

- **More control.** The government has estimated that SEK 15–20 billion is paid out incorrectly from the welfare systems every year, of which about half is suspected to be intentional. The Swedish Social Insurance Agency has therefore expanded risk-based controls, and it has been proposed that the agency should be given its own law enforcement operations.
- **More trust and preventive work.** The new Social Services Act, in force since July 2025, places greater emphasis on preventive work and gives municipalities the opportunity to offer certain interventions without an individual needs assessment. The government’s Trust Delegation worked from 2016 to 2019 to reduce detailed steering in the public sector.

The controls have also raised questions about legal certainty and discrimination. The Swedish Social Insurance Agency used a machine-learning-based risk profile to select cases concerning temporary parental benefit for control. After investigative journalism, the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection initiated supervision in 2025, and the Swedish Social Insurance Agency then stated that the risk profile had been taken out of use.

## The cybernetic reading

People’s needs vary greatly, and a rule-governed authority can only handle part of that variation. According to Ashby’s law, there are in principle two ways to meet it:

- **Strengthen assessment:** give case officers, social workers and local actors more room to adapt support to the individual’s situation.
- **Dampen variation:** use standardised formulas, clear rules and standardised decisions, so that equal cases are treated equally.

Both have costs. More room for assessment gives better adaptation, but also a greater risk that the outcome depends on which case officer one meets. More standardisation gives predictability and equal treatment, but is less suited to unusual situations. Legal certainty requires that equal cases be treated equally, which argues for clear rules in social insurance. Everyday support, such as social services and care, benefits more from local knowledge and flexibility.

## Remaining problems

- **Mental ill-health and sick leave.** Psychiatric diagnoses are the most common cause of ongoing sickness cases for both women and men. This raises questions about how working life, healthcare and insurance interact.
- **Marginal effects.** When income from work reduces means-tested benefits almost krona for krona, it becomes difficult to work one’s way out of benefit dependency. A so-called employment incentive in social assistance mitigates this for some, but the problem remains.
- **Targeted central government grants.** Municipalities receive many temporary central government grants with their own conditions and reporting requirements, which makes it harder to plan long-term and adapt interventions locally.
- **Preventive interventions that no one owns.** An intervention that reduces future costs for several actors, for example social services, healthcare and the police, is difficult to finance when each actor has its own budget.

## Subsidiarity-based proposals

The proposals are ideas to test in limited pilots with evaluation. Each proposal has its own page.

| Proposal | Brief description |
|--------|------------------|
| [Welfare Rings](./valfardsringar) | Neighbourhood networks for mutual help in everyday life, with a link to social services when needs become greater. |
| [Social prescribing](./social-forskrivning) | Healthcare refers patients with, for example, loneliness or mild mental ill-health to associations and activities, with the support of a coordinator. Builds on physical activity on prescription and culture on prescription. |
| [Neighbour verification](./grannverifiering) | Faster processing of small, urgent needs, where the applicant can choose to let a relative or neighbour confirm the need. |
| [Personal welfare budgets](./personliga-valfardsbudgetar) | For long-term recipients: coordinated payments, longer decision periods and better opportunities to work without losing all support. |
| [Municipal welfare budgets](./kommunala-valfardsbudgetar) | Fewer targeted central government grants and greater local room for manoeuvre in pilot activities, with national minimum levels and evaluation. |
| [Social investment funds](./sociala-investeringsfonder) | Funds for preventive interventions that are followed up closely. Several municipalities and regions already have such funds. |

## Objections

| Objection | How the proposals need to address it |
|---------|------|
| Local variation produces inequality | National minimum levels, the right to appeal and open reporting of results are needed, and social insurance should remain national. |
| Less control produces more fraud | Simplifications should be tested with spot checks and evaluation. Control and trust need to be balanced, not set against each other. |
| Neighbours and civil society cannot replace rights | The proposals should complement public interventions, not replace them. The right to assistance should not depend on whether one has a network. |
| Privacy | Proposals in which parties other than the authority become aware of a need require the individual’s consent and clear boundaries. |

## Conclusion

Welfare already has a division of labour that fundamentally follows the subsidiarity principle: national insurance for loss of income, local support in everyday life. The most difficult questions concern the balance between control and trust, and how preventive work is to be financed when the benefit arises for many actors and only after a long time. The proposals here test more local room for manoeuvre and greater participation from civil society, but without making the right to support depend on where one lives or whom one knows.

### Three things you can do

**If you are a municipal politician:** Find out how the municipality is using the new Social Services Act’s possibility to offer interventions without a needs assessment, and whether the municipality has funds for social investments.

**If you work in healthcare:** Investigate whether your region has physical activity on prescription or culture on prescription, and which associations in the local area can receive patients.

**If you are a resident:** Get involved in an association or a neighbourhood network. Informal community is no replacement for rights, but an important complement.
