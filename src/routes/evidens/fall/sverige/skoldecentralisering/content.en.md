# S2 — Swedish School Decentralization / Municipalization

**Country:** Sweden · **Period:** 1989–present, primary reform phase 1990–1993 · **Domain:** Education, local governance, resource allocation, professional autonomy · **Status:** Finished draft

---

## Case at a glance

Sweden's school reforms of 1989–1993 transferred substantial responsibility, employment authority and financial discretion from the state to municipalities — and explicitly intended decentralization to continue *past* the municipality, down to individual schools and professionals. That intention makes this case unusually valuable: it lets us test the "nested centralization" hypothesis (H4) against the reform's own stated design, not against a standard we invented after the fact.

**What actually changed?** Formal responsibility shifted from state to municipality with high confidence. Whether it continued on to schools and professionals, as intended, is much less clear: evidence shows local steering below the municipal level was uneven, and smaller municipalities in particular often used their new formal autonomy only weakly for years after the reform.

---

## Context

Swedish schooling had historically been characterized by strong central regulation. The reform culminating around 1990–1991 was part of a broader shift from detailed state regulation toward **goal- and result-oriented governance**: the state would set national goals and guidelines, while municipalities would organize and implement school activity within those frameworks. This was not framed simply as "give schools to municipalities" — it was a redesign of the whole governance architecture, from detailed central control toward distributed responsibility.

## What was intended?

**Move operational responsibility closer to citizens.** The 1990/91 proposition explicitly argued that the school should be based on students' needs and circumstances, and that increased local influence could not be achieved centrally.

**Clarify responsibility.** The previous arrangement split responsibility between state and municipality. The 1989/90 reform of teacher employment explicitly described the existing "double principalship" as a problem, since neither state nor municipality had fully coherent personnel responsibility.

**Preserve national equality.** The reform explicitly did not intend to abandon national standards — the proposition frames the tension directly as *greater local influence versus a nationally equitable education system.*

Crucially, the government also stated that decentralization should **not stop at the municipal political and administrative level** — individual schools were meant to receive greater independence over their own activities. The intended chain was:

```text
State → Municipality → School → Teacher/professional → Student
```

not simply:

```text
State → Municipal administration → School
```

## What changed formally?

- **1989/90:** State regulation of employment conditions for teachers, school leaders and guidance personnel ended, transferring this to municipal responsibility.
- **1990/91:** The new division was established — the state kept national goals, guidelines, evaluation, financial support and equivalence requirements; municipalities/school principals took organization, resources, implementation, personnel, local development, and local follow-up.
- **Resource system:** State subsidy was redesigned from detailed earmarked funding into a more general financial contribution, increasing municipalities' discretion over resource allocation between activities.

## What changed materially?

This is where the case becomes complicated. IFAU's analysis of school plans across 18 municipalities found that almost all early-1990s plans largely **repeated national goals** rather than substantially adapting them to local circumstances. Larger municipalities became more active over time; many smaller municipalities remained relatively passive throughout the study period. This is direct evidence that **formal decentralization did not automatically produce substantive local differentiation** — local discretion is not self-activating.

---

## Authority topology

The intended architecture built in an important safeguard: **centralization of outcomes does not require centralization of methods** — national authorities specify what the system should achieve, lower levels get discretion over how. That's a potential model of "subsidiarity with national guarantees." But the "how" arrow from formal authority to actual local steering cannot be assumed:

```text
Formal authority ↑ → Potential autonomy ↑ → Capacity/knowledge/incentives? → Actual local steering
```

The final arrow is exactly what the evidence below complicates.

## Information topology

The government argued local actors were better placed to determine necessary measures, priorities, organization and resource allocation — what needs to be done becomes visible in actual school activity and can't be solved entirely centrally. The intended flow was information **upward**, authority **downward**. But later evaluations (the 2014 Schools Commission) found weaknesses in municipal follow-up and evaluation, partly attributed to lack of competence and partly to weak interest in systematic evaluation. This is exactly the distinction the project's model needs: **information locality ≠ information use.** A municipality can be geographically and organizationally closer to schools while still failing to extract, interpret or act on the information available.

## Resource topology

The reform deliberately linked financial responsibility to operational responsibility — the actor responsible for achieving goals should also control the resources to achieve them. That's an important principle: **decision authority without resource authority is incomplete decentralization.** But it also introduces a second problem — local resource autonomy can increase territorial variation, which is why the state simultaneously retained national responsibility for equivalent provision. That tension is directly relevant to H9.

## Capacity topology

One of the strongest dimensions of this case. The 2014 Schools Commission documented weaknesses in municipal steering, follow-up and evaluation; the IFAU study found smaller municipalities considerably less active in using their formal planning instruments. Legal competence, administrative capacity, analytical capacity and professional capacity are not the same thing — a municipality can have the legal authority to decide without the organizational infrastructure to decide well.

## Nested centralization

**Assessment: conditionally supported as a hypothesis, not yet established as a general empirical result.**

The government's own proposition anticipated exactly this problem, stating decentralization should not stop at the municipal level. The evidence shows local steering below the municipality was uneven, and later investigations found municipal responsibility itself became fragmented among different groups and levels. That's consistent with formal decentralization terminating in a new administrative centre — but a stronger claim requires a detailed comparison of authority, information, resources and initiative at the municipality → school → professional → student level, which hasn't been fully reconstructed yet. The defensible formulation: **the reform was explicitly designed to avoid stopping at the municipality, but subsequent evidence indicates this deeper decentralization was incomplete and uneven** — not the blunter "municipalization produced nested centralization."

## Coordination topology

Removing detailed state regulation while retaining national goals and evaluation reduced one kind of coordination burden but introduced another: the new arrangement relies much more heavily on monitoring, evaluation, interpretation, communication and professional development. When those mechanisms were weak, formal decentralization could generate governance gaps — which is part of why the government later identified a need for stronger national steering.

## Observed outcomes

**Student achievement — exceptional caution required.** Swedish school performance declined after the reforms, but IFAU's analysis concludes the decline **began before** the 1990s reforms, which also bundled school choice, independent schools, new grading systems, new upper-secondary structures, and goal/result-oriented management. Causal attribution to municipalization alone is not warranted. What *can* be said: results declined. What *cannot* yet be said: municipalization caused it. Stronger causal evidence exists for a different, more specific mechanism: IFAU found reductions in teacher density during the 1990s were associated with poorer student performance — a resource mechanism, not necessarily a municipalization mechanism.

**Resource allocation.** Skolverket found large variation in school resources between and within municipalities. The original argument was that local knowledge would let municipalities direct resources toward higher-need pupils; later assessments found schools often received limited compensation for socioeconomic differences, and even highly segregated municipalities didn't necessarily allocate resources proportional to need. This is evidence against a *naive* version of H1 (local information → better matching) — but it's equally consistent with weak local analytical capacity, incentives, funding constraints, or segregation, rather than the underlying mechanism being wrong.

**Local steering.** Direct, clean evidence from the IFAU school-plans study: early plans repeated national objectives, local adaptation was limited, and many smaller municipalities stayed relatively passive for years. Local discretion is not self-activating.

## The later correction: renewed national steering

The 2014 Schools Commission concluded the state needed to take stronger responsibility for quality and equivalence. More recently, a 2025 inquiry proposed a national school-funding norm due to substantial differences in municipal school funding — but ultimately considered a *binding* norm inappropriate for now, proposing a *guiding* norm instead. This should not be read as "decentralization failed, so centralization returned." A better reading: **the governance system has repeatedly moved along the spectrum between local discretion and national standardization, trying to reconcile local adaptation with national equity** — almost exactly the project's own governance-fit problem.

---

## Counterevidence / qualifications

- The decline in school results cannot be validly attributed to municipalization alone — it began earlier and coincided with several other simultaneous reforms.
- Official evaluations don't show a uniform failure: large municipalities did become more active over time, so "local autonomy doesn't work" is too strong a claim.
- Weak local steering could reflect capacity, incentives, or interest — not necessarily a flaw in the decentralization mechanism itself.

## What this case teaches us

S2 is not principally evidence that decentralization was a mistake, nor that local government "knows best." Its lesson is architectural: **moving formal responsibility downward does not guarantee that information, capacity, initiative and effective decision-making move with it.** The reform itself anticipated this. That gives the project a recursive test for subsidiarity, applicable at every institutional boundary: *did authority move? did relevant information move? did resources move? did capacity move? did initiative move? does accountability remain legible?* If the answer is "no" at any boundary, formal decentralization may produce only partial subsidiarity — and that is precisely where "nested centralization" becomes empirically testable, rather than a rhetorical label.

Highest-priority next work: mapping actual authority below the municipality (municipality → school → principal → teacher); separating the municipalization effect from simultaneous school reforms; comparing Swedish school governance with Nordic systems combining national standards with different degrees of local autonomy.

**Do not use this case to claim that school municipalization caused Sweden's educational decline.** Use it to test the more precise proposition that effective subsidiarity requires more than transferring formal responsibility — decision authority, information, resources, capacity, initiative and accountability must be aligned across the levels where the actual work happens.
