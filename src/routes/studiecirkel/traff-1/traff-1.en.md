# Meeting 1: How do we encounter the systems?

**Goal:** Participants share experiences of what it is like to encounter society's systems in everyday life, both when they work and when they don't, and begin to think about why that is.

**Materials you need:**
- Refreshments (coffee, tea, something to eat)
- Slips of paper (A5) and pens for everyone
- Flipchart or large paper on the wall
- Printout of the "System Feeling Meter"

---

## 0–10 min: Welcome and check-in

**What you can say:**

> "Welcome, everyone. Thank you for coming. I'm [your name] and I'm leading these four meetings.
>
> We're going to talk about how society works in everyday life: when we go to the health centre, have contact with the school, or need help from an authority. What works well, what works badly, and why?
>
> The material comes from svensksubsidiaritet.se, a project based on an idea called subsidiarity: that decisions should be made as close as possible to those affected, but at a higher level when needed. You don't have to agree with that idea. On the contrary: part of the point is that together we test when it holds and when it doesn't.
>
> We'll meet four times. Today we share experiences. Next time we test a tool for examining a system. The third time we look at examples and at the pros and cons of different ways of organising things. The fourth time we decide whether we want to do something together.
>
> No pressure. You share what you want to share. We start and finish on time.
>
> Let's go around: your name and one word for how it feels to be here tonight. I'll start: [your name], and I'm feeling curious."

**Tips:**
- Say "thank you, [name]" after each person.
- If it goes quiet: wait a few seconds before moving on.

---

## 10–25 min: A few concepts to bring along

**What you can say:**

> "Before we start sharing experiences, I want to give us a few words to think with. They're not answers, but tools."

**Explain briefly and write on the flipchart:**

- **Decision level:** where a decision is made, for example in the school, the municipality, the region, the state or the EU. The municipality is responsible for, for example, schools and elderly care, the region for healthcare and public transport, and the state for, among other things, social insurance.
- **Standardisation:** making things the same for everyone. It provides equivalence and predictability, but doesn't always suit the individual situation.
- **Adaptation:** making things different according to need. It can fit better, but can also lead to equal cases being treated differently.

**What you can say:**

> "Both have their merits. We want everyone to have the same rights, regardless of where they live. At the same time, we want the person helping us to see our particular situation. Much of what we talk about during these weeks is about how to balance the two."

---

## 25–35 min: The System Feeling Meter (individual exercise)

**What you can say:**

> "Now you'll get a list of systems that many of us encounter. Mark how you usually experience them. There's no right or wrong."

**Hand out the System Feeling Meter.** It has a scale from "like a number" to "seen as a person," and a line for writing an example.

**Give 5 minutes of quiet time.**

**After 5 minutes:**

> "Look at your answers. Which systems do you experience best? Which worst? Is there anything that surprises you?"

Let a few share voluntarily. Feel free to ask: "What is it that makes that particular system work well for you?"

---

## 35–65 min: Conversation in the circle

**What you can say:**

> "Now we'll talk together. We'll take three questions and go around once for each. You can always pass. Remember: we're talking about how systems work, not about individual people who work in them."

### Question 1 (10 min): When has a system worked well for you?

- Feel free to start yourself with an example.
- Listen for what made the difference: a person who knew you, clear rules, a quick decision, being able to get hold of someone.

### Question 2 (10 min): When has it not worked?

- If someone gets angry at a particular official: "I understand. What do you think made it so that person couldn't help you? Was it rules, time, resources, something else?"

### Question 3 (10 min): Where was the decision made?

> "Think of a decision that has affected your everyday life, for example a bus route, a change in school or a rule in healthcare. Do you know where the decision was made? Would it have been better or worse if it had been made somewhere else?"

**Tips:**
- It's common not to know where a decision was made. That is itself an important observation.
- Write down the decision levels mentioned on the flipchart.

**Summarise openly:**

> "What I hear is [summarise]. Sometimes the problem seems to be that the decision is made far away. Sometimes it seems to be the opposite, that common rules are missing. Sometimes it's about resources. We'll take that with us to next time."

---

## 65–75 min: Looking ahead to the next meeting

**What you can say:**

> "Next time we'll test a tool called the Proximity Compass. It helps us examine where decisions are made, whose knowledge is used, and how well a system copes with disruptions.
>
> If you want, you can find out one thing before next time about a system you talked about today: who actually decides over it? The municipality, the region, the state or someone else? The municipality's and region's websites usually have the answer."

---

## 75–85 min: Summary

**What you can say:**

> "Today we've shared experiences of what it's like to encounter society's systems. We've seen examples of when it works and when it doesn't, and we've started thinking about whether it has to do with where decisions are made, or with something else.
>
> Next time, [date and time], we'll examine a system more closely. You're welcome to bring a neighbour or colleague."

**Practical:**
- Can everyone come next time?
- Does anyone need a ride or help with childcare?
- Would the group like a shared chat?

---

## 85–90 min: Check-out

> "Last round: one word for how you feel now."

---

## If something goes wrong

**No one talks:** "Let's take 30 seconds and write down a thought first, then we'll share."

**One person dominates:** "Thank you, that was valuable. Now I'd like to hear from someone who hasn't said anything yet."

**Party-political debate starts:** "I hear that many have strong opinions. Today we're trying to understand how the systems work, regardless of which party is in power. What was it in the system itself that made it turn out that way?"

**Someone becomes sad or angry:** "This touches a nerve. Shall we take a short break?"

**Time runs out:** Skip question 3 and go to the summary.

---

## To hand out

**Meeting 1 – summary**

- **Decision level:** where a decision is made (school, municipality, region, state, EU).
- **Standardisation:** the same for everyone – predictable and equivalent, but not always adapted.
- **Adaptation:** different according to need – better adapted, but a risk of unequal treatment.

**For next time (voluntary):** Find out who decides over a system we talked about.

**Read more:** svensksubsidiaritet.se/system-analys

---

## Checklist for the study circle leader

**Before:** refreshments, slips of paper and pens, flipchart, printouts of the System Feeling Meter, chairs in a circle, time and place confirmed.

**During:** start on time, check-in, concepts, System Feeling Meter, three questions, looking ahead to next time, check-out, finish on time.

**After:** note down what worked, remind the group two days before the next meeting.
