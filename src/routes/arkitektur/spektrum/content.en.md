# The Governance Spectrum

## From Parliamentary Government to Polycentric GGF Governance

The central question is: how much does a society need a unified government as its primary steering mechanism? And, more deeply: which allocation of decision rights, knowledge, coordination, implementation and accountability produces the best results under different conditions?

Sweden's current architecture starts from a clear central structure — but negative parliamentarism already gives it some institutional tolerance for minority rule. The interesting move is to treat "no majority" not as a problem to be solved, but as a **design parameter**.

The spectrum is not a simple line. Several dimensions can move independently: a system can become more participatory without becoming more decentralized, more expert-driven without becoming less democratic, more polycentric without giving up a strong national executive.

---

## The twelve architectures

![The Governance Spectrum](/images/architecture/governance-spectrum.en.svg)

| # | Architecture | Core idea | Divergence from today |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Majority Coalition | A coalition with its own majority governs with a coherent program | Very low |
| 2 | Minority Government | The government governs without a majority, as long as it is tolerated | Very low |
| 3 | Confidence-and-Supply | Parties secure the government's survival and budget, otherwise independent | Low |
| 4 | Issue-by-Issue Parliamentarism | Majorities are assembled separately for each policy | Moderate |
| 5 | Managerial / Neutral Executive | Parliament decides; a less partisan executive implements | Moderate |
| 6 | Parliament + Citizens' Assemblies | Representative democracy supplemented by randomly selected deliberative bodies | Moderate |
| 7 | Multi-Council Governance | Different institutions represent different kinds of legitimacy and knowledge | High |
| 8 | Domain-Polycentric Governance | Different policy domains have partly autonomous, coordinating ecosystems | High |
| 9 | Deep Subsidiarity | Decisions migrate systematically to the lowest competent level | Very high |
| 10 | Nested Polycentric Governance | Local, regional and national institutions are recursively coupled | Very high |
| 11 | GGF-style Plural Governance | Democratic, ecological, local and expert institutions jointly govern | Extremely high |
| 12 | Adaptive Polycentric Commonwealth | Governance becomes a continuously learning, dynamically reconfigurable system | Maximum |

The important thing is that architecture #12 isn't simply "more decentralized" than the rest. It changes the very ontology of what governance is — the question shifts from *who governs?* to *how does the system continuously determine who should govern what, at which scale, using what knowledge, under which conditions?*

---

## Four independent axes

The whole spectrum can be described with four axes that move partly independently of one another.

**Political concentration** — from a single governing coalition, via minority rule and plural parliamentary coalitions, to multiple autonomous decision centres.

**Geographic concentration** — from national, via multilevel governance and subsidiarity, to nested/polycentric governance.

**Epistemic concentration** — from politicians alone, via politicians plus experts, to multiple interacting epistemic communities.

**Institutional adaptability** — from fixed constitutional roles, via periodic reform, to feedback-driven, adaptive governance architecture.

An architecture can move along one axis without moving the other three. That is why the spectrum is best understood as a **family** of architectures rather than a single ruler running from "government" to "no government."

---

## Four decentering moves

Across the whole spectrum, four successive decenterings take place — we call them decenterings rather than decentralizations, since not all four are geographic:

1. **Decenter political aggregation.** From "one coalition controls the whole agenda" to "different issues can generate different legitimate majorities."
2. **Decenter epistemic authority.** From "elected politicians are the primary knowledge channel" to "citizens, experts, affected communities, civil society and data contribute through differentiated institutional channels."
3. **Decenter territorial authority.** From "the nation is the default level" to "authority follows problem scale through subsidiarity and nested governance."
4. **Decenter governance design itself.** From "the architecture is largely fixed" to "institutions coordinate, learn and periodically redesign how responsibilities are allocated."

The fourth move is the one that leads directly into **[meta-governance](/arkitektur/meta-styrning/)** — and it can, in fact, be wise to strengthen it *early*, as a safeguard for the first three.

---

## A note of caution

Scores and rankings are easy to over-interpret. The research gives **no** support for the claim that polycentric governance automatically outperforms centralized governance — coordination costs and accountability problems can become substantial, and outcomes are highly context-dependent. The spectrum is therefore primarily a tool for **asking better questions**, not a ranking where a higher number means "better."

Our most concrete hypothesis for Sweden, in fact, is not to aim for architecture 12. The most interesting reform zone may well sit around **architectures 4–6**: a stable executive, more fluid parliamentary majorities, institutionalized citizen deliberation and stronger expert feedback — without needing to redesign the entire state.
