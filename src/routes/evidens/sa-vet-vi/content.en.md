# How We Know

## Our epistemic method

Before we present a single conclusion, we need to be explicit about **how we know what we think we know**, and how confident we actually are.

This may be the most important page in the whole Evidence section, since it sets the rules for everything else.

---

## What counts as evidence?

We distinguish between several types:

- **Empirical evidence** — observed outcomes in real cases or data.
- **Causal evidence** — studies designed to isolate a causal effect.
- **Observational evidence** — patterns and associations without strong causal identification.
- **Case studies** — reconstruction of what actually happened in a specific reform or institution.
- **Formal models** — mathematically precise results within an explicit model.
- **Simulations** — computational experiments based on a model.
- **Experiments** — controlled or field interventions (we don't have any of our own yet — see below).

None of these is automatically better than the others. They answer different questions, and a good conclusion usually draws on several types together.

---

## Claim levels: [R] / [IP] / [H]

We use the same terminology as the Governance as Engineering (GAE) series to keep track of how certain a claim actually is:

**[R] Rigorous**
A result established within a specified formal model, theorem, established result, or a suitably controlled numerical experiment.

**[IP] In Principle**
A disciplined translation from a formal result to an institutional interpretation.

**[H] Heuristic**
Exploratory, illustrative, approximate, or weakly grounded.

The most important thing to understand: **[R] does not automatically mean "true of the real world."** A result can be entirely rigorous within its model and still say little about actual institutions, if the model's assumptions don't match reality. That distinction is exactly what lets us use GAE's formal work without over-interpreting it.

---

## Confidence

Beyond the claim level, we often assess a separate **confidence rating**:

- **High**
- **Medium**
- **Low**
- **Unresolved**

Confidence is about how much evidence there is and how consistent it is — not about how formally rigorous it is.

---

## Mechanism fidelity

How closely does the evidence actually test the mechanism the hypothesis claims?

- **High** — the evidence tests the mechanism directly.
- **Partial** — the evidence tests a related mechanism, but not all critical components.
- **Low** — the evidence is relevant mainly by analogy or broad association.

A study can be methodologically excellent yet still have low mechanism fidelity for our specific question, if it measures something that only partially overlaps with the mechanism we care about.

---

## Why we reconstruct cases instead of trusting labels

"Decentralization" is a label, not a mechanism. A reform can formally decentralize responsibility while actual decision-making authority, information, resources and initiative remain centralized.

That's why every **[case](/evidens/fall/)** builds its own map of what changed **formally** and what changed **materially** — rather than accepting a reform's own rhetoric.

---

## What would change our minds?

Concrete examples of evidence that would weaken, modify, or falsify important hypotheses in our framework:

- Consistent evidence that local decision-making does **not** improve information use even when autonomy and capacity are high.
- Systematic evidence that intermunicipal cooperation performs **worse** than both merger and independent provision, across multiple functions.
- Robust evidence that citizen participation produces **no** measurable capability gains, even in well-designed deliberative processes.
- Repeated cases where functional scale-matching does **not** correlate with better outcomes.

Actively looking for such evidence matters as much as gathering supporting evidence. See **[Counterevidence →](/evidens/syntes/motevidens/)**.
