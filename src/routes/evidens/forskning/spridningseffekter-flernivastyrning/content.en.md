# Spillovers & Multilevel Governance

## What happens when a problem's causes, effects or dependencies cross institutional or geographic boundaries?

---

## At a glance

**Evidence status:** Conditionally supported
**Confidence:** Medium
**Key finding:** When a policy problem's natural boundary doesn't match the governance boundary handling it, coordination problems tend to persist even after the responsible level is reassigned — the boundary itself needs bridging mechanisms, not just reassignment.
**Coverage note:** This topic's primary evidence base is one case (S3); it should be read as an initial pattern, not a settled finding.

---

## The question

Some problems stay neatly within one jurisdiction. Many don't — a patient's care needs, a river's pollution, a labour market, or a housing shortage rarely respect administrative lines. This topic asks:

> When effects, causes or dependencies spill across governance boundaries, what determines whether coordination succeeds or breaks down?

---

## Key mechanisms

- **Spillovers** — effects of a decision that fall partly or wholly outside the deciding jurisdiction.
- **Boundary fit** — how well a governance boundary matches the actual functional or geographic extent of a problem.
- **Bridging mechanisms** — joint committees, information-sharing protocols, coordinated planning requirements, and similar structures that connect otherwise separate principals.
- **Principal fragmentation** — when responsibility for interdependent parts of the same problem is split across more than one legally distinct decision-maker.

---

## What the evidence shows

**[Ädelreformen (S3)](/evidens/fall/sverige/adelreformen/)** is the clearest case in the corpus. The reform deliberately kept physicians within the county healthcare system while transferring most other elder care to municipalities — creating exactly two co-equal principals meeting at a shared patient. The government's own 2003 review found that, despite predominantly positive overall outcomes, significant coordination problems persisted specifically for people with complex or rapidly changing needs — precisely where the boundary was crossed most often. The system's response is itself informative: Socialstyrelsen issued 1996 regulations on information transfer at hospital discharge, a 1999 inquiry examined cooperation specifically, and Prop. 2002/03:20 introduced joint committees and mandatory coordinated care planning. That sequence — boundary problem → local workarounds → formal guidance → structural bridging mechanism — is a real-world instance of a system building coordination infrastructure *after* observing failure at its own interfaces, rather than solving it by simply redrawing the boundary.

---

## Where findings agree

- A governance boundary can be reasonable for organizational or specialization purposes while still being poorly aligned with the functional trajectory of the problem it's meant to serve.
- Coordination problems at a boundary don't necessarily indicate the boundary was wrong — they can indicate the *bridging mechanisms* across it were insufficient.

## Where findings diverge

- The corpus doesn't yet have a comparative case where a similar boundary problem was solved primarily through *reassignment* (moving the whole function to one principal) rather than *bridging* (adding coordination mechanisms while keeping split responsibility) — so we can't yet compare the two strategies directly.

---

## Counterevidence

- Ädelreformen's overall evaluation was predominantly positive despite the persistent coordination problems — this case does not support "split responsibility always fails."
- Confounding changes to the broader healthcare system during the study period (hospital-bed reductions, rising medical complexity in municipal care) complicate attributing coordination problems purely to the boundary design.

---

## Our assessment

*This is our interpretation, distinguished from the source-derived findings above.*

The single case in this corpus is consistent with **[C5 — coordination is a design variable, not merely a cost](/evidens/syntes/vad-vi-vet/)**: when authority is split across principals for interdependent parts of a problem, the quality of the bridging architecture — not just the initial boundary choice — determines whether spillovers get resolved or persist. This is only a single-case pattern; it needs testing against additional cases with different boundary structures before it can be treated as a general finding.
